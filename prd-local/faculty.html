Introduction to the Faculty module

What this module is

The Faculty module is the teacher's side of Shikho Campus. It is the single place where a university teacher prepares a course, meets the class, publishes what the class must read or watch, sets the work the class must do, judges that work, keeps the attendance register, and hands a defensible set of final grades to the department. Everything a teacher is professionally accountable for lives here, and nothing a teacher is accountable for lives anywhere else.

That last sentence is the design of the whole product in one line. A university runs on the credibility of its marks. If a mark can be created by a clerk in the exam office, or quietly nudged by a piece of software, or published to a student before the teacher intended, the institution loses the one thing it sells. So the module is built around a very narrow ownership rule: the teacher is the author of academic judgement, and every other party — the administrator, the coordinator, the artificial-intelligence helper, the student — is either a reader of that judgement or a supplier of the facts the teacher judges upon.

Around that core, the module is deliberately ordinary. It does the unglamorous work that consumes a teacher's week in a Bangladeshi private university: repeating the same announcement on three sections, chasing the twelve students who did not submit, rebuilding last semester's course from scratch because there was no way to copy it, marking eighty scripts in four days because results close on Thursday, and answering the same question about the midterm syllabus forty times. The module's promise is not that teaching becomes exciting. It is that the administrative weight around teaching drops far enough that the teacher can spend the recovered hours on the part that actually needs a human.

Who uses it

"Faculty" is not one person. At a private university in Dhaka, Chattogram or Sylhet the same course may be touched by six kinds of staff in a single semester, and the module has to fit all of them without pretending they are the same. A full-time professor with a research load, a lecturer carrying five sections, an adjunct who arrives at seven in the evening after a full day at a bank, a lab instructor who never lectures but marks every lab report, a graduate teaching assistant who may see submissions but may never post a mark, and a course coordinator who owns the shared shape of a multi-section course — each needs a different door into the same room.

Two of these roles carry restrictions that the module enforces rather than merely documents. A teaching assistant can do almost everything a marker does and cannot do the one thing that matters — release a mark. A course coordinator can shape a course and cannot reach into a colleague's gradebook. Where a person holds two roles at once, which is common in small departments, the module resolves rights per course rather than per person: the same individual may be the coordinator of one course and merely a section teacher in another, and the screens change accordingly.

A day in the life

It is a Tuesday in the seventh week of the Spring semester. Rumana is a lecturer in the Computer Science department, teaching two sections of an introductory programming course and one section of a database course. She wakes at half past six, and before she is out of bed she opens the app on her phone. The dashboard tells her three things in one glance: her first class is at eight-thirty in room 610 and the room camera is online, forty-one of her fifty-three students have submitted the assignment that closed at midnight, and the artificial-intelligence helper has prepared draft feedback for thirty-eight of those forty-one scripts and is waiting for her to look at them. She does not look at them yet. She taps the twelve non-submitters and sends a single message reminding them that the late window closes on Thursday and costs them ten per cent. That takes forty seconds and it is the first useful thing she has done today.

She reaches campus at ten past eight, and starts the class from the podium computer. She does not open a separate meeting product, hunt for a link, or paste anything into a group chat; she opens today's session and presses start, the lecture-hall camera comes on, the seven students who are attending online join within a minute, and attendance begins recording from that moment. She teaches for eighty minutes. Halfway through she runs a two-question poll because the room has gone quiet, and it tells her that a third of the class has not understood indexing, so she spends ten more minutes on it and drops the last example. When she ends the class, the recording is already attached to this week's topic in the course, and she does not have to remember to upload anything.

Between classes she sits in the staff room with tea and works the marking queue on her laptop. The queue does not show her a list of students; it shows her one script at a time, with the AI's draft mark and draft comment already filled in, the marking scheme beside it, and the student's name hidden because her department turned on anonymous marking. She agrees with the draft on the first four, edits the comment on the fifth because the AI missed that the student had solved the problem in a smarter but unrequested way, and rejects the draft entirely on the eighth because the AI has misread handwriting in a scanned page. Each one she approves takes about twenty seconds; the ones she has to think about take three minutes. Nothing she has approved is visible to any student yet, because her course is set to hold marks until she posts them together — she will do that on Friday, when all eighty are done, so that no student learns their mark before their neighbour and starts a conversation she cannot yet answer.

At two o'clock she has an advisee appointment. The student is in her fifth semester with a grade point average that has slipped for two terms running, and the advisee screen shows Rumana the whole picture before the student sits down: the falling average, two courses currently at risk, an unpaid second instalment, and an attendance figure in one course that is three sessions away from making the student ineligible for the final exam. They talk for twenty minutes about dropping one course. Rumana writes four lines of notes; the notes are visible to the next advisor who ever meets this student, and never to the student herself.

At four she has a departmental job rather than a teaching one. The chair has asked all three teachers of the introductory course to submit midterm questions to a shared paper, and Rumana has been given two questions on loops. She writes them, marks them as her contribution, and cannot see the questions the other two teachers submitted — that is the point of the exercise. On the way home she gets a notification that her database section's average on the last quiz is well below the other two sections of the same course, which is exactly the sort of thing she would not have noticed until results week. She makes a note to open the item analysis tomorrow. That is the whole day: perhaps five hours of teaching and judgement, and about forty minutes of administration, where the same week two years ago would have cost her four hours of administration and produced worse records.

What this module owns, and what it never owns

Most arguments in a university information system are ownership arguments in disguise. The table below is the answer we will keep returning to for every feature on this page.

The Faculty module owns thisThe Faculty module never owns this
The academic judgement. Every mark, every piece of feedback, every decision that a piece of work is worth what it is worth. A mark exists because a named teacher decided it exists. The roster. Who is in the class comes from the university's own student records. A teacher never invites a student, never shares a joining code, and never removes a student from a class list. If the roster is wrong, the fix happens in the registrar's office and flows back here.
The teaching material. The modules, topics, readings, slides, recordings and syllabus of the course, and the decision about when each becomes visible to students. The calendar. Semester dates, examination weeks, holidays, add-and-drop windows and result deadlines are set by the university. The module obeys them and warns when a teacher's own dates conflict with them.
The assessment design. What work is set, how much each piece is worth, how late work is treated, and when marks become visible to students. Money. No fee, waiver, fine or refund is visible to or changeable by a teacher, with one deliberate exception: an advisor is told that an advisee has a finance hold, because it changes the advice, but is never shown the amount.
The attendance record. Who was present, who was absent, who was excused, and the evidence for each — whether it came from a physical roll call or from a recorded join in an online class. Certification. Submitting final grades is where the teacher's authority ends. The department chair certifies or returns them. The teacher cannot certify their own results, and the chair cannot alter them.
Advising notes and supervision records. The professional record of what was discussed with a student and what was recommended. Institutional policy. Grade scales, attendance thresholds, weightage limits, plagiarism rules and academic-integrity outcomes are configured by the university. The module enforces them; it does not soften them for anyone.
The three lines nobody may cross An administrator never edits a mark — the chair may certify a set of results or return it to the teacher with a reason, and nothing else. The artificial-intelligence helper only drafts — no draft mark and no draft comment has any academic effect until a named teacher approves it, and there is no way to approve a batch without seeing it. Teachers never invite students — class lists come from the university's student records, so there are no joining codes, no invitation links and no self-enrolment anywhere in the product.

How this module connects to the rest of Shikho Campus

The Faculty module is the noisiest producer in the platform. Almost everything a student sees was authored by a teacher, and almost everything an administrator reports on was measured from teaching activity. These are the three relationships that matter, and each is described again, feature by feature, further down the page.

The teaching day

Three features carry a teacher from waking up to walking out of the lecture hall. They are used more often than everything else on this page put together, so they are specified first and in the most detail.

FAC-HOME-01 · Teaching dashboard

P0

What it does. The teaching dashboard is the first screen a teacher sees after signing in, and it answers one question: what needs me today. It gathers the teacher's next class, the work waiting to be marked, the deadlines closing this week, the students who have started to slip, and anything the department has asked of them, and it puts all of it in one ranked list instead of five separate places. It is deliberately a summary and not a workplace — nothing is finished here, but everything that must be finished today is visible here and one tap away. A teacher carrying five sections and three hundred students should be able to look at this screen for eight seconds on a phone and know exactly where their next hour goes.

Why it exists

Teachers do not lose time because any single task is hard. They lose it because the day is fragmented and nobody assembles it for them. In the systems most Bangladeshi private universities run today, a lecturer must open one place to see the timetable, another to check whether an assignment has closed, another to see how many students submitted, another to find out that the chair wants midterm questions by Thursday, and a fourth to discover that a student has stopped attending. Each check costs two minutes and a context switch, and the checks that get skipped are always the ones about students, because students do not send calendar invitations.

The second reason is emotional rather than logistical. A marking backlog that is invisible feels infinite. Teachers put off marking not because eighty scripts is a lot but because they cannot see the eighty as a finite, shrinking thing. A dashboard that says plainly "sixty-two scripts remaining, about two and a half hours at your usual pace, results close in four days" converts dread into a plan. The same is true of risk: "four students have not submitted anything in three weeks" is an item a teacher will act on in a minute if it is put in front of them, and will never discover on their own until the student fails.

The third reason is that the dashboard is the only place in the module where information from every other module is allowed to meet. The registrar's calendar, the coordinator's roster changes, the chair's deadlines and the students' behaviour all converge here. Everywhere else the module stays inside one course; here it deliberately steps back.

What the user sees

Greeting and day line

The teacher's name, today's date, and a single sentence summarising the day: how many classes, how many items need attention. On a phone this is the only thing above the fold besides the next class.

"Good morning, Rumana. Three classes today and sixty-two scripts waiting."
Next class card

The most prominent element on the screen. Course code and title, section, time, room or online, today's topic if the teacher has planned one, and the state of the room equipment if a classroom camera is assigned. A single primary action starts the class; it becomes available fifteen minutes before the scheduled time and stays available until the session's end.

"Start class"
Needs your judgement

The marking backlog, grouped by assessment rather than by student, with a count, an estimated time based on the teacher's own past pace, and a hard deadline if one applies. "Assignment 3 · CSE 115 Section 4 · 62 to mark · 38 AI drafts ready for your review · results close Thursday"
Closing soon

Deadlines in the next seven days that belong to the teacher: assignments closing, quizzes opening, final grade submission dates, and anything the department has requested such as question paper contributions. Each row states who is waiting and what happens if the date passes.

"Midterm question contribution · requested by Chair, Computer Science · due in 2 days"
Students who need a look

A short, honest list — never more than five entries — of students showing a pattern rather than a single event: three consecutive absences, no submission for three weeks, a sharp fall in performance, or attendance approaching the eligibility threshold. Each entry names the course and offers one action, usually to send a message or open the advising record.

"Tanvir Ahmed · CSE 115 Section 4 · absent 3 classes in a row · 2 more absences until he is ineligible for the final"
Today's remaining classes

The rest of the day's timetable as a compact strip, with rooms and any change flagged in colour — a room moved, a class cancelled by the university, a makeup class added.

My courses

Every section the teacher holds this semester as a card: enrolled count, content readiness, marking outstanding, and whether the course is published to students. The path onwards is Faculty → My courses → the course.

Coordination strip (coordinators only)

Visible only to a teacher who coordinates a multi-section course: which sections have not published this week's content, which are behind on marking, and where a section has drifted from the master course.

How it works, step by step

  1. 1
    TeacherSigns in and lands on Faculty → Dashboard. Nothing is chosen, filtered or configured; the screen is the same every day so that the teacher's eye learns where each thing lives.
  2. 2
    SystemGathers the day from the university timetable, the teacher's own courses, the marking backlog, the deadlines that apply to this teacher, and the student patterns computed overnight. Anything that cannot be gathered in time is shown as a quiet placeholder rather than an error, because a slow attendance service must never stop the teacher from starting class.
  3. 3
    SystemRanks what it found. Ranking is fixed and explainable rather than clever: a class starting within the hour outranks everything; a deadline today outranks a deadline this week; a student at risk of losing exam eligibility outranks a student who merely missed one submission. The teacher can never be surprised by the order.
  4. 4
    TeacherLooks at the next class card. If class is imminent they start it here and the dashboard hands over to the live class feature; if not, they read down the screen.
  5. 5
    TeacherOpens the marking backlog for one assessment. The dashboard hands over to the grading queue with that assessment already selected, so no filtering is needed.
  6. 6
    TeacherActs on a closing deadline — for example, opening the question contribution the chair requested, or extending an assignment that half the class has not started.
  7. 7
    TeacherActs on one at-risk student, usually by sending a short message from the dashboard itself. The message is recorded against the student so a future advisor can see that the teacher reached out.
  8. 8
    SystemRefreshes the screen as the day moves. A completed class disappears from the next-class slot and the following class takes its place; a cleared backlog collapses to a single line of confirmation rather than vanishing silently, so the teacher gets the satisfaction of seeing it done.
  9. 9
    SystemAt the end of the day, replaces the greeting line with a short summary of what was completed and what has carried over to tomorrow.

What must never happen

  • The dashboard must never show a mark to anyone before the teacher has posted it. Counts of unmarked work are safe; a preview of an unposted mark is not, because the dashboard is frequently open on a projector in a shared staff room.
  • It must never show a teacher a student who is not in one of their sections or on their advisee list. A risk flag is sensitive, and a teacher's legitimate interest ends at their own roster.

  • It must never offer any action that would create or change a mark. Every path from the dashboard into judgement work goes through the grading queue, where approval is explicit.

FAC-LIVE-01 · Take a live class, from your own device or the classroom camera

P0

What it does. This feature runs the actual class. The teacher starts a session either from their own laptop or phone, or from a camera fixed in the lecture hall, and students join from wherever they are — in the same room, at home, or on a bus. Attendance is recorded from the moment each person joins, the recording begins automatically unless the teacher turns it off, and everything produced during the class attaches itself to the right course and the right week without anyone uploading anything afterwards. There is no separate meeting product, no link to copy into a group chat, and no joining code, because the class list already knows who belongs in the room. The teacher flow is Green Room (checks camera, slides, and roster) then Go live. Students get raise-hand, chat and polls. Adaptive quality drops gracefully on weak connections.

Why it exists

Almost every Bangladeshi private university now teaches in some mixture of physical and online, and almost all of them do it by bolting a consumer meeting tool onto an academic system that knows nothing about it. The consequences are the same everywhere. The link is shared in a messaging group, so anyone who has ever received it can join, including students from a different section and people who are not students at all. Attendance is taken by scrolling a participant list and typing names into a spreadsheet. The recording lands in someone's personal cloud storage and is shared by a link that expires or that the next teacher cannot find. When the semester ends, the university has no record of which classes actually happened.

The academic cost is larger than the administrative one. Attendance in these universities is not a formality — it commonly determines whether a student may sit the final examination at all. If attendance is captured by hand from a participant list, it is wrong, and a wrong attendance record either bars a student who attended or admits one who did not. Both outcomes end up in the chair's office.

There is also a practical reason specific to this market. A great deal of teaching happens from lecture halls fitted with a fixed camera and a podium computer, where the teacher is not carrying a laptop at all and cannot be expected to sign in to a personal account in front of sixty students. At the same time, evening adjunct faculty teach entirely from a laptop at home on a domestic connection. The same feature must serve both without either feeling like a compromise, which is why the mode choice is the first thing on the screen rather than a setting buried in preferences.

FAC-REC-01 · Record and share lectures

P0

What it does. This feature takes everything that was recorded — whether it came out of a live class or was recorded alone in an office at eleven at night — and turns it into something a student can actually use. It files the recording under the right week of the right course, produces a written transcript so the content can be searched, makes a small version that plays on a weak mobile connection, and lets the teacher trim the dead minutes at the beginning before anyone sees it. The teacher decides when it becomes visible and for how long, and can see afterwards how many students watched and where they stopped. Nothing is ever uploaded to a personal cloud account or shared by a link that outlives the semester. Publishing can be quiet: the recording is saved to the course library and tagged to curriculum topics without notifying every student. An untagged recording is simply a general session — the surface never implies a missing action. Students get a synced searchable transcript, clips, and Smart Class (AI-SMART-01).

Why it exists

Recording a class is the easy part; every meeting tool does it. What no meeting tool does is put the recording where a student will look for it. In practice a recording made outside an academic system goes into a personal drive, gets a share link, and the link goes into a group chat where it is buried under two hundred messages by Friday. Three weeks later a student trying to revise for the midterm cannot find week four, and the teacher cannot remember which folder it was in. By the next semester the recordings are gone entirely, because the teacher who made them has moved on or has run out of storage.

The second reason is bandwidth and honesty about it. A full-quality recording of a ninety-minute class is a large file, and a student on a metered mobile connection in a district town will not watch it. If the only version available is the big one, the feature exists for the students who least need it. A small version and an audio-only version are not conveniences; they are the difference between the feature working and not working for a substantial part of the class.

The third reason is that a recording is the most reusable thing a teacher makes. A well-recorded explanation of a hard topic is good for years. But it is only reusable if it can be found by topic rather than by date, which is why a transcript matters — a student searching for "normalisation" should land on the eleven minutes of week six where it was explained, not be told to watch four ninety-minute videos.

Finally, there is a governance reason. Universities are increasingly asked, by accreditation bodies and by their own quality offices, to show that teaching happened and what was taught. A library of recordings that belongs to the institution rather than to individual teachers' personal accounts is the only honest answer to that question.

Course content

Six features cover the making of a course: building it, rebuilding it from a previous run, keeping many sections aligned with one master version, deciding what students may see, publishing the syllabus, and telling the class things. This is where a teacher spends the fortnight before a semester and about an hour every week during it.

FAC-CNT-01 · Course content and modules

P0

What it does. This is where a course is built. The teacher organises the semester into weeks or themed units, and inside each one places the things students must read, watch, do and discuss — slides, notes, links, recordings, assignments and quizzes — in the order the students should meet them. Each item can be visible now, visible from a date, or hidden while it is still being written. The result is what a student sees when they open the course: not a pile of files with cryptic names, but a path through the semester that says what to do first.

Why it exists

The most common shape of a university course in an existing system is a folder of files. Twenty-eight items called things like "Lecture 4 final v2 revised.pdf", uploaded in whatever order the teacher happened to find them, with no indication of which week they belong to or whether the student was supposed to read them before or after the class. Students cope by asking each other, which means the well-connected students cope and the others do not. A course that is merely a file store shifts the work of organising the semester from one teacher onto fifty students, fifty times over.

Structure also matters to the teacher, and for a reason that becomes obvious only in week nine. A course organised as weeks is a course whose progress can be seen. The teacher can tell at a glance that they are two weeks behind the plan, that week six has a reading but no recording, and that the assignment they thought they had set is still sitting hidden. A folder of files tells them nothing, so the drift is only discovered when the syllabus runs out of time.

The third reason is reuse, which is the quiet economic argument for the whole content feature. A course that has structure can be copied to next semester, pushed to seven other sections, handed to a new teacher, and audited by the quality office. A folder of files can only be zipped and emailed.

The final reason is that content and assessment must live in the same place. The moment an assignment lives in a different part of the system from the material it assesses, students miss it. Putting the assignment inside week seven, immediately after the reading it depends on, is not decoration — it measurably changes how many students submit on time.

FAC-CNT-02 · Import or copy a previous course

P1

What it does. This feature rebuilds a course from something that already exists — the same course as the teacher ran it last semester, a colleague's course they have been given permission to use, or a departmental template. The teacher chooses what to bring across and what to leave behind, and every date in the copied material is moved forward to the new semester automatically, so an assignment that was due in week seven last spring is due in week seven this spring. Student work, marks and discussions are never copied; only the teaching material and the shape of the course come across. What arrives is a complete draft course that the teacher then adjusts, rather than an empty shell they must fill again.

Why it exists

Rebuilding a course from nothing at the start of every semester is the single largest piece of wasted work in university teaching, and it is entirely avoidable. A lecturer teaching the same introductory course for the sixth time should not spend a weekend re-uploading the same slides and retyping the same fourteen topic names. In practice they do, and the version they rebuild is slightly worse each time, because some material is lost and some dates are wrong.

Dates are the part that people underestimate. Copying material is easy; copying a course whose assignment deadlines, quiz windows and release dates all land on the right days of a different semester is not. A teacher who copies material without dates has saved half the work and left themselves the fiddly half. This feature is only worth building if it moves the dates properly, including handling the fact that a semester might be a week shorter, that public holidays fall differently, and that Ramadan and Eid shift by eleven days a year and change the teaching pattern of the whole term.

There is also an equity argument inside a department. When a strong teacher builds an excellent course, the department should be able to give that course to the new adjunct who arrives three days before the semester starts. Without import, the adjunct builds something thin from scratch and the students in that section get a worse semester than the students in the section next door. With import, the floor rises.

FAC-CNT-03 · Master course sync to sections

P2

What it does. When one course runs in many sections with different teachers, this feature lets a coordinator hold a single authoritative version of it and push changes into every section. The coordinator decides which parts are locked — typically the syllabus, the weightage and the common assessments — and which parts each teacher may adapt to their own class. When the master version changes, every section receives the update without losing the local work its teacher has added. Unlike importing, which is a one-time copy, this is a standing relationship that lasts the whole semester.

Why it exists

A large introductory course at a private university may run in eight or ten sections taught by six different people, several of them part-time. The department is answerable for those sections being the same course. Today they achieve that with a shared folder and a meeting, and by week nine the sections have drifted: one has covered two extra topics, one has set a different midterm weightage, and one is using last year's syllabus. When students compare marks — and they always compare marks — the department has no defensible answer.

Copying the course into each section at the start does not solve this, because courses change during a semester. A question is found to be defective, a reading is replaced, a deadline moves after a hartal. Without a sync relationship, the coordinator must contact six teachers and hope all six make the same change on the same day. Two of them will not, and nobody will notice until results week.

The hard part, and the reason this feature is rated below the others, is that a naive sync destroys teachers' work. A lecturer who has added three of her own worked examples to week five will not tolerate a system that deletes them because the coordinator republished the master. The design therefore has to distinguish, item by item, between what the coordinator owns and what the teacher owns — and it has to do so in language a teacher understands without training.

FAC-CNT-04 · Publish content and preview as a student

P0

What it does. This feature is the single answer to the question "can students see this yet". Every week, item, assignment and area of a course has one visibility state — visible, hidden, or visible from a date — and this feature is where a teacher sets it, sees it all at once, and checks it. The checking half matters as much as the setting half: the teacher can walk through their own course exactly as a student in it would see it, with everything hidden actually hidden, before anyone else looks. It also controls which parts of the course exist for students at all, so a course that never uses discussions does not show students an empty discussion area.

Why it exists

Two opposite accidents happen constantly in university systems, and both are serious. The first is a teacher publishing something too early: the midterm question paper visible three days before the exam, the answer key attached to an assignment that has not closed, next week's quiz reachable by anyone who guesses the address. The second is a teacher believing they published something when they did not: fifty students unable to find the assignment, emailing at eleven at night, and a deadline that has to be extended.

Both accidents come from the same root cause — the teacher cannot see what the student sees. Every system has some notion of publishing, and most of them scatter it: a visibility switch here, a release date there, an availability window somewhere else, and a separate rule for whether the module is published. A teacher cannot hold four rules in their head while preparing a course at midnight, so they guess, and the guess is what produces both accidents.

The answer is to make visibility one idea and then to make it verifiable. Preview as a student is not a nice extra; it is the verification step that makes the whole content system trustworthy. A teacher who can spend thirty seconds walking their course as a student will catch the exposed answer key that no amount of careful switch-setting would have caught.

FAC-SYL-01 · Syllabus

P1

What it does. The syllabus is the course's contract with its students, and this feature is where it is written, published and kept honest. It holds the course description and learning outcomes, the week-by-week plan, how the final mark is composed, the attendance and late-work rules, the books and materials, and how to reach the teacher. Most of it is filled in from information the university already holds or the teacher has already entered elsewhere, so the teacher writes the parts only they can write. Once published it is always one tap away for a student, and any change to it is visible as a change rather than a quiet edit.

Why it exists

Almost every dispute between a student and a teacher is a disagreement about what was promised. How much is the midterm worth. Was late submission allowed. Was attendance part of the mark. Which chapters are in the final. In a well-run department the answer is in the syllabus; in most departments the syllabus is a document that was distributed as a file in week one, exists in three versions, and cannot be found by anybody in week twelve. The chair then adjudicates from memory.

The second reason is that a syllabus is duplicated information, and duplicated information goes stale. The weightage in the syllabus document and the weightage actually configured in the gradebook are typically written twice by the same person on different days, and they disagree surprisingly often. When they disagree, the student has a legitimate grievance and the department has no defence. Building the syllabus from the same settings the system actually enforces removes the possibility entirely.

The third reason is external. Accreditation bodies and the University Grants Commission ask for course outlines, learning outcomes and their mapping to programme outcomes. Departments currently assemble these by emailing teachers and pasting documents together, once every few years, in a panic. A syllabus that is structured from the start turns that exercise into a report rather than a project.

FAC-ANN-01 · Announcements

P0

What it does. This is how a teacher tells a class something. It sends a message to one section, to several sections at once, or to a chosen group such as everyone who has not submitted, and it delivers through the app, the notification and — where the university allows it — a text message for genuinely urgent things. The teacher can see afterwards how many students actually read it, which is the part that changes behaviour. It exists so that teaching communication happens inside the course, where it is permanent and reaches everyone, rather than in a messaging group where it reaches whoever is scrolling.

Why it exists

Right now, class communication in most Bangladeshi private universities happens in a social messaging group created by a class representative. The teacher may or may not be in it. Nobody knows who is in it. Students who joined late are not in it. Messages are buried within hours. When a teacher writes "tomorrow's class is moved to room 512", perhaps sixty per cent of the class sees it, and the teacher has no idea which sixty per cent. If a student misses an examination because they never saw a message, the university's position is indefensible.

The second reason is repetition. A teacher with four sections of the same course currently writes the same message four times, or writes it once and forgets a section. Making one message go to several sections at once, while still appearing to each student as a message about their own course, removes a genuine weekly irritation.

The third reason is targeting. The most useful message a teacher sends is rarely to the whole class — it is to the twelve people who have not submitted, or the eight whose attendance is close to the threshold. Sending that message to everyone is noise that trains students to ignore announcements; sending it individually is thirty minutes of work nobody does. A targeted announcement takes forty seconds and is the single highest-value action on the dashboard.

Assessment design

Five features decide what students are asked to do, what each piece is worth, what being late costs, when marks become visible, and how one student can be given different conditions without disturbing the rest. Everything here is decided before any work is marked, and most of it before the semester begins.

FAC-ASM-01 · Create assignment

P0

What it does. This is where a teacher sets a piece of work. They write what students must do, decide what students may submit — a file, typed text, a link, or work done as a group — set when it is due, say what it is worth and which part of the final mark it counts towards, and describe how it will be marked. Once published it appears inside the right week of the course, in the students' work list, and in the teacher's own gradebook, all as the same single thing. The marking scheme written here is what the grading queue and the artificial-intelligence helper will use later, which is why a few extra minutes spent here saves hours in week ten.

Why it exists

An assignment set badly is expensive at every later stage. If the instructions are vague, a third of the class submits the wrong thing and the teacher marks thirty arguments instead of thirty answers. If the accepted file types are not stated, submissions arrive in six formats and two of them will not open. If there is no marking scheme, the teacher marks the first ten scripts more generously than the last thirty and cannot explain the difference to a student who complains. If the assignment is not attached to a part of the final mark, the gradebook cannot compute anything and somebody rebuilds the arithmetic in a spreadsheet in week fifteen.

There is also a specifically Bangladeshi consideration that shapes the design. Many students submit from a phone, photographing handwritten work, on a connection that fails halfway through an upload. An assignment feature that assumes a laptop and a stable connection will produce a stream of "my submission did not upload" disputes that the teacher must adjudicate without evidence. So the submission side must be forgiving and must produce a receipt, and the teacher's side must show clearly what arrived and when.

Finally, the marking scheme deserves its own argument. Teachers resist writing one because it feels like paperwork. But a marking scheme is what makes the artificial-intelligence helper useful rather than dangerous — a draft mark produced against explicit criteria can be checked in twenty seconds, while a draft mark produced against nothing must be re-marked from scratch. The scheme is also the teacher's defence when a student challenges a mark. The feature therefore makes writing one fast and optional-but-encouraged rather than a compulsory form.

FAC-ASM-02 · Create a quiz or online exam

P0

What it does. This feature builds a test that students take on screen. Questions can be multiple choice, true or false, short answer, long written answer, numerical, matching, or a file upload for work done on paper. The teacher sets how long students have, how many attempts they may make, whether questions and options are shuffled so no two students see the same order, and whether questions are drawn at random from a bank so that no two students get the same paper. Multiple-choice answers are marked the moment the quiz closes; written answers go into the marking queue for the teacher. The same feature builds a five-minute class quiz and a two-hour online midterm — the difference is only in the settings.

Why it exists

Quizzes are the most frequent assessment in a semester and the most tedious to run on paper. A teacher running a weekly ten-question quiz on paper for three sections spends an hour photocopying and three hours marking, every week, for work that a machine can mark perfectly. Recovering those four hours a week is the largest single time saving available to a teaching-heavy lecturer, and it is the reason this feature is rated alongside grading itself.

The harder requirement is the online examination. Since the pandemic every university in this market has run examinations online at least sometimes, and most expect to keep the option for makeup examinations, for evening programmes, and for the weeks when the campus cannot open. An online examination has needs a class quiz does not: a hard time limit that survives a browser crash, question shuffling and random draws so that neighbours cannot copy, a way to give one student extra time without giving it to everyone, and an honest record of what happened if a student's internet fails eleven minutes before the end. Building two separate features for these two cases would guarantee that the important one is the less-tested one, so they are the same feature.

The third reason is the question bank. A teacher who has written four hundred good questions over six semesters owns something valuable, and in most systems it is trapped inside individual quizzes and lost at the end of the year. Questions here belong to the course rather than to the quiz, so they accumulate, can be tagged by topic and difficulty, can be reused, and can be analysed — a question that everybody gets right or everybody gets wrong is telling the teacher something.

FAC-ASM-03 · Grade groups and weightage

P0

What it does. This is where a teacher decides the shape of the final mark: that quizzes are worth twenty per cent, assignments fifteen, the midterm twenty-five, the final thirty and the lab ten. Every piece of work is placed in one of these groups, and the gradebook uses them to compute each student's running and final total. The teacher can also say how many of the weakest quizzes to drop, whether a group must be passed on its own, and whether attendance carries marks. It is a small screen that determines every number in the course, which is why it is set before any work is marked and changed afterwards only with a warning.

Why it exists

Every university course in this market already works this way on paper. The syllabus says quizzes twenty per cent, midterm twenty-five, and so on. What is missing is a system that knows it. In practice the teacher enters marks somewhere, and in week fifteen exports everything to a spreadsheet, builds the weighted total by hand, and submits the result. That spreadsheet is where the errors live: a column referenced wrongly, a student's row misaligned, a quiz counted twice, a percentage that adds to a hundred and five. These errors are invisible until a student complains, and they are very hard to disprove afterwards.

The second reason is that students cannot see where they stand unless the system knows the weightage. "You scored fifteen out of twenty" means nothing to a student; "you are at seventy-two per cent overall and the final is worth thirty" is actionable. Running standing is one of the most valued things the Student module offers, and it depends entirely on this screen being filled in.

The third reason is departmental consistency. Where a chair requires every section of a course to use the same weightage, that requirement can only be enforced if weightage is structured data rather than a sentence in a document. Making it explicit also makes drift visible, which is the coordinator's whole job.

FAC-ASM-04 · Late policy and grade posting policy

P1

What it does. This feature settles two questions once for a whole course instead of arguing about them piece by piece. The first is what a late submission costs: whether late work is accepted at all, for how long, and what penalty applies. The second is when a mark becomes visible to a student: the moment the teacher enters it, or only when the teacher deliberately releases all of them together. Both are stated to students in advance, applied by the system rather than by the teacher's memory, and recorded so that a dispute in week fourteen can be settled by looking rather than by remembering.

Why it exists

Late work is the most common point of friction between students and teachers, and almost all of that friction comes from inconsistency rather than from strictness. A teacher who accepts one student's late assignment because they asked nicely, and refuses another's because it was the end of a long week, has created a grievance they cannot defend. A policy declared in advance and applied by the system is fairer than any teacher's judgement in the moment, and it also removes dozens of small negotiations from the teacher's week.

Grade posting is less discussed and more important. When a teacher marks eighty scripts over four days and each mark appears the instant it is entered, several bad things happen. The first ten students know their marks before anyone else and start discussing them. A student who sees a mark the teacher intends to revisit treats it as final. A teacher who realises after script forty that they have been marking too harshly cannot correct the first thirty-nine without visibly changing marks students have already seen. Above all, marks trickle out in an order determined by the teacher's marking sequence, which students inevitably read as favouritism.

Holding marks until the teacher releases them together solves all of this and costs nothing. It also gives the teacher room to be human — to change their mind, to re-mark, to moderate a whole cohort after seeing the spread — without any student being aware that a number ever moved. This is the single most requested behaviour from experienced teachers, and the single most surprising omission from most systems.

FAC-ASM-05 · Exam moderation — extra time or an extra attempt for one student

P1

What it does. Sometimes one student needs different conditions from the rest: a student with a documented disability who is entitled to extra time, a student whose connection failed halfway through an online examination, a student who was in hospital on the day and needs a fresh attempt, or a student sitting a makeup examination in a different week. This feature gives that one named student more time, another attempt, or a different window — with a reason recorded — while every other student's conditions stay exactly as they were. It is deliberately narrow, deliberately visible, and deliberately impossible to apply to a whole section by accident.

Why it exists

Every examination has exceptions, and the way they are handled today is informal. A teacher tells a student to start late and remembers to let them finish late. A student's internet fails and the teacher reopens the quiz for everyone for ten more minutes because that is the only control available, which quietly advantages the whole section. A student with a documented entitlement to extra time either does not get it, because the system cannot do it, or gets it through an arrangement that leaves no record.

All three are failures of the same kind: an individual decision made without a mechanism, which means without evidence. When a student later complains that another student got more time, the teacher has nothing to show. When an accreditation review asks how disability accommodations are implemented, the department has anecdotes.

There is a fairness argument in the other direction too. Because reopening for everyone is the only tool most systems offer, teachers reasonably avoid granting accommodations at all, which harms exactly the students the accommodation exists for. Giving the teacher a precise instrument makes generosity safe.

FAC-GRD-01 · Grading queue with AI assistance

P0

What it does. This is where marking happens. Instead of a list of students to work through, the teacher is given one piece of work at a time, with the marking scheme beside it and — where the university has enabled it — a draft mark and draft comment already prepared by the artificial-intelligence helper. The teacher reads, agrees or disagrees, edits whatever they wish, and approves. Approving is the only act in the entire product that creates a mark. Until a teacher approves, the AI's draft has no academic existence: no student can see it, no total includes it, no report counts it, and no process can turn it into a mark by any other route.

Why it exists

Marking is the largest single consumer of a university teacher's time and the least protected. A lecturer with three sections of fifty students marks a hundred and fifty scripts for every assessment, and there are perhaps eight assessments in a semester. At six minutes a script that is a hundred and twenty hours of marking in fourteen weeks, on top of teaching. The consequences are predictable and universal: marking is done in exhausted batches at night, feedback shrinks to a number and a tick, the last thirty scripts get less attention than the first thirty, and results are submitted at the deadline with nothing left over for the students who most needed a comment.

An assistant that drafts is genuinely transformative here, because most marking is not judgement — it is recognition. Most scripts are ordinary: they do roughly what was asked, and the marker's job is to confirm that against a scheme and write a sentence. A draft that says "this meets criteria one and two fully, misses the third, suggested fourteen out of twenty, here is a comment" turns a six-minute task into a twenty-second confirmation. The time saved goes to the scripts that are genuinely difficult, which is exactly where a teacher's attention belongs.

But an assistant that decides would destroy the university. A mark is an institutional act with legal and professional weight; it determines degrees, scholarships and careers. It must be attributable to a qualified human being who can be asked to defend it. This is not a matter of accuracy — it would remain true if the AI were more accurate than the teacher, because the accountability, not the accuracy, is the point. So the architecture of this feature is one sentence: the AI proposes, a named teacher disposes, and nothing crosses the line between those two things.

That is why there is no bulk approval. A button that approves forty drafts at once is functionally identical to letting the AI mark, and everyone involved knows it. The teacher must see each draft. What the feature owes the teacher in return is that seeing each one should take seconds, not minutes — which is a design obligation on us, not a compromise on the rule.

The line that defines this feature A draft is not a mark. No student sees it, no total includes it, no report counts it, and no action anywhere in the product converts a batch of drafts into marks. A mark comes into existence at the moment a named teacher approves it, and the teacher's name stays attached to it for as long as the record exists.

FAC-GRD-02 · Gradebook and submitting final grades to the chair

P0

What it does. The gradebook is the whole picture of a section: every student down the side, every assessment across the top, the weighted total and the letter grade at the end. It computes totals from the course's own weightage rather than from a spreadsheet, shows what is missing before it becomes a problem, and lets the teacher see how the class as a whole has performed. At the end of the semester it does one more thing, which is the most consequential action in the module: the teacher submits the final grades, the section locks, and the result set goes to the department chair to certify. From that moment the teacher cannot change a mark, and neither can the chair — the chair may only certify the set or return it with a reason.

Why it exists

The gradebook is where a semester's work either adds up or does not. Today, in most departments, the adding up happens in a spreadsheet built by hand in the last week of the semester, and it is the least reliable step in the entire academic process. Marks are copied from several places, columns are weighted by formulas nobody checks, a student who joined in week three has a blank that becomes a zero, and the result is submitted as a printed sheet that the chair signs without any means of verifying it.

Beyond arithmetic, teachers need to see the shape of a class before they commit to it. Is the average where it should be. Is there a cluster just below a grade boundary. Did the section that meets at eight in the morning do measurably worse than the one at eleven. These are the questions a conscientious teacher asks in the last week, and they can only be answered if all the marks are in one place with the weightage applied.

The submission step exists for a reason that is about institutional integrity rather than convenience. In a paper process there is a moment when a teacher's judgement becomes the university's record, and that moment is a signature. It matters that this moment exists, that it is deliberate, and that after it the teacher can no longer change anything quietly. It matters equally that the person receiving the results cannot change them either. The most common corruption risk in a university is not a teacher marking badly; it is a mark being altered after the teacher has finished. So the certification step gives the chair exactly two powers — accept the set, or send it back with a written reason — and no third.

FAC-ATT-01 · Attendance register

P0

What it does. The register records who was present at every session of every section. It combines two sources — the automatic record of who joined an online class, and the teacher's own roll call for students in the room — into one honest register where the evidence for each mark is always visible. It shows each student's running percentage against the university's requirement, warns the teacher and the student well before a student becomes ineligible to sit the final examination, and handles the excused absences that a medical certificate or a university duty produces. It is the least glamorous feature in the module and, in this market, one of the two or three that decides whether a student graduates on time.

Why it exists

At most Bangladeshi private universities attendance is not advisory. A student below the required percentage — commonly seventy or seventy-five per cent — may be barred from sitting the final examination, which means failing the course regardless of how well they were doing. That makes the attendance register a document with the power to cost a student a semester and a considerable amount of money, and it is currently kept on paper, in a notebook, or in a personal spreadsheet.

The consequences of that are exactly what you would expect. The register is not available to the student until it is too late to act. Disputes are unresolvable because the evidence is one person's handwriting. A student who attended every class but whose name was called while they were outside for two minutes cannot prove anything. And the teacher, who took the register conscientiously, has to defend it in the chair's office from memory in week fifteen.

The second reason is the online class. When a class is taken online, presence is a genuinely new kind of evidence: an exact join time, an exact leave time, and a total connected duration. This is better evidence than a roll call, and it should be captured automatically. But it is also easy to misuse — a student whose connection failed for ten minutes should not lose their attendance, and a student who joined and walked away should not gain it. So the register must hold the raw evidence, apply the university's rule to it, and let a human override with a reason.

The third reason is early warning. A student who is going to be barred is identifiable by week eight, and almost nobody tells them. Warning a student at seventy-eight per cent that they have two absences left changes behaviour; telling them at sixty-eight per cent that they are barred changes nothing.

Beyond the classroom

Four features cover the parts of an academic's job that are not a course: guiding advisees through a degree, contributing to departmental examinations, supervising a thesis over a year, and reading what students said about your teaching.

FAC-ADV-01 · My advisees

P1

What it does. Almost every teacher at a private university is also the academic advisor to twenty or thirty students, and this is where that job lives. It lists the teacher's assigned advisees with the whole picture for each one: their degree progress, their current grades, their attendance, whether anything is blocking their registration, and whether they are showing signs of trouble. It handles appointments the student books, the questions they send, and the private notes the advisor writes for the record. It exists so that an advising meeting starts with the advisor already informed, rather than with twenty minutes of the student explaining their own situation.

Why it exists

Academic advising is the highest-leverage half hour in a student's semester and the worst-supported. The advisor typically meets the student having seen nothing, asks them what courses they have taken, believes the answer, and signs a registration form. Meanwhile the information that would have changed the conversation entirely exists in the university's systems and is not in the room: the student has failed the prerequisite twice, their average has fallen for three terms, they are barred from an examination on attendance in one course, and they have an unpaid instalment that will block registration the moment the advisor signs.

The second reason is continuity. Advisors change — a teacher leaves, an advisee is reassigned, a student changes programme. Without a record, every new advisor starts from nothing and the student retells a story they may not tell accurately. A short note written after each meeting, visible to whoever advises this student next, is the cheapest possible improvement in advising quality.

The third is early intervention. The teacher who can see that a student's average has fallen two terms running, that they have stopped submitting in two courses, and that they missed the last four classes is looking at a student who will drop out in about six weeks. Someone has to notice, and the advisor is the only person in the institution whose job it is.

FAC-EXM-01 · Question paper and exam duty support

P2

What it does. Departmental examinations are not built by one teacher. A common midterm across eight sections is assembled from questions contributed by several teachers, reviewed by a moderator, approved by the chair, and kept confidential until the moment it is sat. This feature runs that process: it collects each teacher's contribution without letting them see anyone else's, gives the moderator a way to review and balance the paper, and keeps a strict record of who saw what and when. It also shows each teacher their examination duties — which halls they are invigilating and which scripts they must mark — because those arrive by circular today and are constantly missed.

Why it exists

Question paper handling is the most security-sensitive process in a university, and it currently runs on personal email and printed envelopes. Teachers email questions to the chair's personal account. The chair assembles the paper in a word processor. It is printed at a shop near campus. Every step of that chain has leaked at some university in this market, and when a paper leaks the examination is cancelled, thousands of students resit, and the institution's reputation takes a public hit.

The mundane failure is just as costly. Teachers do not know they were meant to contribute until the deadline passes. The chair chases six people by phone. Two contributions arrive in incompatible formats. The paper ends up unbalanced because nobody could see the whole thing until the day before. And invigilation duties, published as a list on a noticeboard or in a group message, are missed often enough that halls start late.

This feature is rated below the teaching essentials because many departments will run their first semesters on the existing process. But it is in scope from the beginning because a university that has adopted the platform for teaching will not tolerate its most sensitive process staying on personal email for long.

FAC-RSH-01 · Thesis and project supervision

P2

What it does. Supervising a final-year thesis or capstone project is a relationship that runs for two or three semesters, and it does not fit inside a course. This feature gives it a home: the milestones a student must reach, the drafts they submit and the comments the supervisor returns, the record of meetings held, the panel and the defence, and the final assessment that eventually becomes a grade. It works for a single student with one supervisor and for a group project with a supervisor and a co-supervisor, and it keeps the whole history so that a defence panel meeting the student for the first time can see how the work developed.

Why it exists

Thesis supervision today runs on personal email and memory. Drafts are exchanged as attachments and version four is called final version two. Meetings happen or do not happen and nobody records which. A student who is drifting is invisible until the defence approaches and it becomes an emergency. When the panel meets, they see a finished document with no evidence of how it was produced, which makes it hard to judge either the student's contribution or the quality of supervision.

There is also a departmental problem. A chair cannot see how many students each teacher supervises, which of them are behind, or which supervisors are unresponsive. Supervision load is one of the most unevenly distributed burdens in a department and one of the least visible.

The final reason is integrity. As generative tools become ordinary, a thesis submitted as a finished document proves very little. A thesis with a visible history — an outline in month one, a rough chapter in month three, comments and revisions along the way — is evidence of a process, and the process is what the degree is actually certifying.

FAC-FDB-01 · See student feedback results

P2

What it does. At the end of every semester students evaluate their courses anonymously, and this feature is where the teacher reads the result. It shows the numerical ratings for their own sections with the department average beside them for context, the written comments with abusive material screened out, and how their ratings have moved over previous semesters. It is released only after final grades are certified, so that no teacher can connect a comment to a mark and no student can fear that criticism will cost them. It exists to help a teacher improve, which means it must be honest, must be safe for the student, and must not become a stick.

Why it exists

Course evaluations are collected almost everywhere and used almost nowhere. Students fill in the form because a system makes them, and the results go to the chair, or into a drawer, or into a promotion file the teacher never sees. The teacher — the one person who could act on the feedback — often receives a single average months later with no context and no comments. Nothing improves, and students learn that the exercise is pointless, so response rates fall and the data becomes worthless.

The timing question is the one that decides whether the whole exercise is honest. If a teacher can see comments before submitting grades, students know it and write accordingly, and the feedback becomes flattery. If a student thinks a critical comment might be traced back to them, they write nothing. Both failures are avoided by the same rule: results are released only after grades are certified, and identity is never available to the teacher at any point, for any reason, including at the teacher's request.

The context question decides whether it is useful. An average of four point one means nothing on its own. Four point one against a department average of three point six is a compliment; against four point six it is a signal. And a movement from three point two to four point one over three semesters is the most useful thing a teacher can be shown about their own practice.

The teacher's ratings across previous semesters for the same course, which is the most useful view for anyone genuinely trying to improve.

"Students consistently praise your examples and your availability. The most common concern is the time taken to return marked assignments."

"Only 6 of 41 students responded. These results are shown for your information but should not be read as representative."

What must never happen

FAC-AI-01 · Teaching assistant AI

P1

What it does. The teaching assistant is a helper that works alongside the teacher everywhere in this module: drafting marks and feedback in the grading queue, suggesting quiz questions from the week's material, turning a scribbled instruction into a properly worded announcement, summarising thirty student comments into two themes, and answering a teacher's question about their own course. It never acts by itself. Everything it produces is a draft that a named teacher reads and either uses, edits or discards, and until that human decision happens the draft has no effect on any student, any mark, any record or any message. It is deliberately narrow — it works from this course's own material and this university's own policies rather than from the open internet — and it says plainly when it does not know. This is Assistant — the faculty mode of Shikho Campus AI, built in-house on the same approach Shikho already runs in Teacher OS, not a third-party product. Product rules: AI-AST-01 and AI-GRD-01. Private faculty Momentum, if enabled, is never visible to the administrator.

Why it exists

The honest argument for this feature is arithmetic. A lecturer in a Bangladeshi private university teaches four or five sections, sees two to three hundred students, and has perhaps twenty hours a week left after teaching for everything else: preparation, marking, advising, departmental duties and their own development. In those twenty hours they must mark eight assessments for every section, write feedback, build quizzes, prepare content and answer several hundred messages. It does not fit, and what gets dropped is always the same thing — feedback quality, which is the part that actually teaches.

A helper that drafts changes that arithmetic. Not because it teaches, and not because it judges, but because most of the volume in a teacher's week is recognition rather than judgement: recognising that this script does what was asked, that this question is a duplicate, that these thirty comments say two things. Recognition is exactly what these systems are good at, and confirmation is much faster than creation. The recovered hours go to the twenty per cent of work that genuinely needs a professional.

The equally honest argument for the constraints is institutional. A university sells credibility. If the market comes to believe that marks at a Shikho Campus university are produced by software, the value of every degree from that university falls, and no amount of accuracy in the software repairs that. This is not a technical risk to be mitigated; it is a category boundary to be enforced. Hence the shape of the feature: the assistant may prepare anything and decide nothing, and the product contains no mechanism — no setting, no bulk action, no automation, no support tool — that lets a draft become a decision without a named human in between.

There is a third argument that gets less attention and matters over years. A helper that is grounded in the course's own material is a helper that improves as the course improves. It drafts feedback in the language the teacher uses, against the marking scheme the teacher wrote, referring to the lecture the teacher gave. A general-purpose assistant would produce plausible text about the subject; this one produces text about this course, which is what makes it checkable in seconds rather than needing to be verified from scratch.

The rule that defines this feature The assistant drafts and a teacher approves. No draft mark, draft comment, draft announcement, draft question or draft summary reaches a student, enters a record or leaves the platform without a named teacher having seen it and chosen to use it. There is no bulk approval, no automatic acceptance, no scheduled release of unreviewed output, and no configuration that creates one.

Uncertainty is stated in the same place as the answer, not buried in a disclaimer, and the assistant declines rather than guessing when it has nothing to go on.

What it will not do

Requests outside its boundaries are refused with the reason, not with a generic apology, so a teacher learns the shape of the tool in one interaction.

"I cannot post marks. I can prepare drafts for you to review in the grading queue."
"I cannot tell you which students wrote which course evaluation comments. Those are anonymous."
Turning it off

A teacher can switch the assistant off for a course, or for one kind of work such as marking, at any time, with no consequence. Nothing in the product performs worse because the assistant is off; it merely takes longer.

"Assistant is off for CSE 115 Section 4. You will mark without drafts."

A teacher can see where the assistant has been used in their courses and what happened to each draft — accepted, edited or rejected. The same summary, without individual detail, is available to the department.

"This semester: 284 drafts prepared · you accepted 41%, edited 46%, rejected 13%."

  1. 1
    SystemUses only what it is entitled to: this course's content, its marking schemes, its recordings and transcripts, this section's own results, and the university's policies. It does not use another teacher's course, another section's marks, or anything about a student outside this course.
  2. 2
    TeacherWorks normally. The assistant offers something relevant to the work in front of them rather than waiting to be summoned.
  3. 3
    SystemProduces a draft, marks it clearly as a draft in the record itself as well as on the screen, and states how confident it is and what it based the draft on.
  4. 4
    TeacherReads it. Accepts, edits, or rejects it. This is the only step that has any effect on anything.
  5. 5
    SystemRecords the outcome — accepted, edited or rejected — against the draft, and keeps the draft beside whatever the teacher finally decided.
  6. 6
    SystemUses the pattern of edits to improve later drafts in the same course, so that the assistant gradually learns this teacher's standards rather than staying generic.
  7. 7
    SystemMonitors rejection rates. Where the assistant is being rejected often for a particular kind of work, it stops offering drafts for that work and tells the teacher why rather than continuing to waste their attention.

    TeacherCan at any moment see what the assistant used to produce a draft, which is what makes checking it fast.
