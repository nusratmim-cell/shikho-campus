The Student module

Everything a university student does between the day they are admitted and the day they collect their certificate: joining class, watching what they missed, submitting work, seeing marks, paying fees, clearing holds, meeting an advisor, and asking for a transcript.

Introduction to the Student module

Who the student is

The student in this document is an undergraduate or postgraduate enrolled at a private university in Bangladesh. She is typically between eighteen and twenty-six years old, attends a campus in Dhaka, Chattogram or Sylhet, and lives with family or in a hostel some distance from that campus. She owns a mid-range Android phone and uses it for almost everything; she may also borrow a laptop in the library or a friend's room when she has heavy work to submit. Her mobile data is metered and her home connection, when she has one, is shared and slow. She is used to a semester rhythm — Spring, Summer and Fall — with an advising period at the start, an add and drop week just after, a midterm around the seventh week, and finals at the end.

She is not one kind of student. Some pay their own fees from a part-time job, some are on a merit waiver, some are supported by a sibling working abroad who sends money at unpredictable times. Some are on academic probation and watching their grade point average nervously; some are in their last semester and thinking only about clearance and convocation. Some read English comfortably; some read Bangla far more comfortably and translate the interface in their head. The module must work for all of them on the same screen.

Her single most common context is: standing somewhere, phone in hand, three minutes free, needing one specific answer. Where do I have to be, what do I owe, what is due, did the mark come out. Every design decision in this module bends towards answering that in one glance.

The question this module answers

Every module in Shikho Campus exists to answer one question well. The Faculty module answers what do I have to teach and mark today. The Administrator module answers is the institution running correctly. The Coordinator module answers are the records ready, and what must I hand to whom.

The Student module answers a different question: what do I have to do next, and what is stopping me?

Both halves of that sentence matter. The first half is the ordinary work of a term: attend class, watch the recording, submit the assignment, sit the exam, read the mark. The second half is the part that Bangladeshi private-university students actually lose semesters to: an unpaid instalment that blocks registration, an unsigned advising approval, an unreturned library book, an admit card that will not download three days before the exam. A student system that only shows coursework and hides the blockers is a half-built product. This module treats blockers as first-class content, gives them their own centre, shows them on the home screen, and always names the exact office and the exact action that clears them.

The mental model

Think of the module as a single desk with four drawers, sitting on top of a truth that lives elsewhere.

Drawer oneLearning — classes, recordings, materials, discussion, timetable. Everything a teacher publishes.
·
Drawer twoAssessment — submissions, marks, standing, exam information. Everything a teacher judges.
·
Drawer threeMoney — dues, payments, instalments, receipts. Everything finance decides.
·
Drawer fourAdministration — advising, registration, holds, documents, clearance. Everything an office signs.

The desk itself owns nothing. It is a very good window onto records that other modules author. A mark belongs to the teacher who posted it. A charge belongs to the finance office that raised it. A hold belongs to whichever office placed it. A degree requirement belongs to the catalogue the registrar published. The Student module reads all of these faithfully, arranges them so a nineteen-year-old can act on them, captures the student's own actions — a submission, a payment, a booking, a request — and hands those actions to whichever module owns the consequence.

What this module owns, and what it never owns

It owns

  • The student's own actions: joining a class, submitting work, starting a payment, booking an appointment, asking a question, requesting a document, answering a survey.
  • The presentation and ordering of everything the student must know — what is urgent, what is blocking, what is merely upcoming.
  • Proof for the student: submission receipts, payment receipts, a record of her own class joins, and a copy of every request she raised.
  • The honest explanation of any refusal — why an action is blocked, which office to approach, and what will unblock it.

It never owns

  • It never authors a mark. No screen, no button, no background process in this module can create, change or reveal a mark that a teacher has not posted. The module displays marks; the Faculty module makes them.
  • It never holds money. Payment goes from the student's wallet or card directly to the university's own merchant account. The platform is never a stop on the way and never keeps a balance for a student.
  • It never invents a hold. Holds are rendered, not created. If the library has not flagged an overdue book, no hold appears; if the library removes the flag, the hold disappears. The module never guesses that a student "probably" owes something.
  • It never overrides policy. Refund percentages, attendance thresholds, eligibility rules and deadlines come from the university's configuration. The module enforces them; it does not soften them.
The three lines nobody may cross A mark is visible only after the teacher posts it. Money moves from student to university and never rests with the platform. A hold shown to a student is a hold some office actually placed, with that office named on the screen.

Getting started

Two features carry the student from an admission letter to a working daily tool. If these fail, nothing else is ever seen.

STU-ONB-01 · First login & orientation

P0

In one line. The first fifteen minutes: sign in with the university email, confirm who you are, walk through a short orientation checklist, and walk away holding a digital identity card. As a newly admitted student, I want my first login to end with me knowing my student number, my advisor's name, my first class and my first payment date — not with a blank dashboard I do not understand.

What this feature does

This feature converts an admitted person into an active student. Before it runs, the student exists only as a record created by the admissions and registry side of the university: a name, a programme, a student identity number, a university email address. She has probably received that email address on a printed slip at the admission desk, or in a text message. She has never opened it. She does not know what a learning management system is, and she has been told by a senior that "everything is on the portal" without being told what the portal is.

The feature is used exactly once per student in the ordinary case, in the first week of the first semester, often on a phone while sitting in an orientation hall with two hundred other new students on the same crowded campus wireless network. That context sets the design constraints: it must work on a phone, it must tolerate an unreliable network, and it must be finishable in a few minutes without anybody standing over the student's shoulder. It also runs a second time in an unhappy path — when a student returns after a semester's break, or when a device is lost and the account is re-verified.

The problem it solves is the single largest cause of a dead student account: a first login that succeeds technically but fails humanly. The student signs in, sees an empty screen because the term has not started, closes the app, and never returns. By the time she needs the platform — the night before her first assignment is due — she has forgotten the sign-in method and cannot recover it. The orientation checklist exists to guarantee that her first session contains at least four concrete, memorable facts and one artefact she keeps: the digital identity card.

The digital identity card matters more in Bangladesh than a Western product manager would assume. Campus gate security, library issue desks, laboratory sign-in registers, examination halls and student discount schemes all ask for an identity card, and the printed card frequently arrives weeks after classes begin. A card on the phone, showing photograph, name, student number, programme, current semester and validity, closes that gap. It is a view of the university's record, not a credential the module invents — the module renders what registry issued.

What the student sees

Sign-in screen

University crest and name at the top, so the student is sure she is in the right place and not a look-alike page. One primary button, "Continue with university email", and one line explaining what that means. Below it a quiet link, "I do not know my university email", which opens help rather than a dead end.

Sign in with the email address your university gave you. It usually ends with your university's own domain, not gmail.com.
University sign-in handover

The student is handed to the university's own sign-in page — the same one used for university email. She types the credentials she was given and is returned to the app. Nothing about the password is seen, stored or handled by the platform.

Welcome and identity confirmation

Her full name in large type, her photograph if registry supplied one, her student identity number, programme, department, admission semester and current semester. Two buttons: "Yes, this is me" and "Something is wrong". Fields she may correct herself — mobile number, alternate email, emergency contact, present address — are editable. Fields only registry may change — name spelling, date of birth, programme, student number — are shown as plain text with a short note.

Your name, date of birth and student number come from the university's official record. If any of these are wrong, raise a correction request — we cannot change them here.
Photograph step

If registry has no photograph, the student is asked to take or upload one, with guidance: plain background, face clearly visible, no cap or sunglasses. The photograph is submitted for registry approval, and the card shows a placeholder until approval lands.

Orientation checklist

Five items with tick marks that fill in as she completes them. Each item is a single tap that opens the relevant screen and returns her to the checklist. In order: meet your advisor, see your class timetable, see your first payment date, learn how to join a live class, and turn on notifications. A progress line reads "2 of 5 done" and the checklist can be left and resumed later from the home screen.

Five short steps. You can leave and come back — we will keep your place.
Notification permission step

Before the phone's own permission box appears, one screen explains what notifications will actually be used for, in specifics rather than promises: class starting in ten minutes, a mark posted, a payment due in three days, a hold placed on your record. A "Not now" option is offered and does not block completion.

We will tell you when a class is about to start, when a mark is posted, when a payment is due, and when something blocks your registration. Nothing else.
Digital identity card

A card-shaped view with the university crest, photograph, name, student number, programme, department, current semester and a validity date. A button saves it to the phone so it opens without a network. A refresh line shows when it was last updated. If the record is not yet active — fees unpaid at admission, or registry approval pending — the card shows a clearly marked "Not yet active" state instead of a valid card, and names the office to visit.

Card valid for Spring 2026. Show this at the campus gate, the library desk and the examination hall. It updates itself each time you open the app.
Finish screen

A short summary repeating the four facts she now knows: advisor's name, first class day and time, first payment date and amount, and where to find help. One button, "Go to my home screen".

How it works, step by step

  1. 1
    UniversityAdmissions and registry create the student's record with name, student number, programme, admission semester and university email address. Until this exists, no sign-in is possible.
  2. 2
    StudentOpens the app or the web address, sees the university crest, and taps "Continue with university email".
  3. 3
    SystemHands her to the university's own sign-in service — the same one that guards university email — and waits. The platform never sees or stores her password.
  4. 4
    StudentEnters her university email address and password on the university's page and is returned to the app.
  5. 5
    SystemMatches the returned identity to exactly one student record. If a match is found, the session begins. If no match is found, the student is shown a clear message naming the registry office rather than a technical error.
  6. 6
    StudentReviews the welcome screen, corrects her mobile number and emergency contact, and confirms "Yes, this is me".
  7. 7
    SystemSaves the editable contact details immediately and records that identity was confirmed, with the date and time. Any request to change a protected field becomes a correction request for registry, not a change.
  8. 8
    StudentUploads a photograph if none exists, then works through the five orientation items, each of which opens a real screen rather than a picture of one.
  9. 9
    SystemMarks each checklist item done as it is genuinely visited, keeps the progress if she leaves, and offers the unfinished checklist from the home screen for the first thirty days.
  10. 10
    StudentReads the notification explanation and allows notifications, or declines and continues.
  11. 11
    SystemBuilds the digital identity card from the registry record and saves a copy on the device so it opens without a network.
  12. 12
    StudentReaches the finish screen, reads the four facts, and taps through to her home cockpit.
  13. 13
    SystemRecords that orientation was completed and stops showing the checklist prompt on the home screen.

What it connects with

  • Administrator module — student records. Every fact on the welcome screen and the identity card is read from the record that admissions and registry created. Name, student number, programme, department, admission semester and photograph flow one way only, from the Administrator module into this screen. Corrections requested here become correction requests in that module's queue.
  • The university's own sign-in service. The platform asks the university "is this person who they claim to be" and receives an answer plus an email address. No password ever reaches the platform, so when the university disables an account — a withdrawal, a dismissal, a graduation — access to Shikho Campus ends with it and no separate cleanup is needed.
  • STU-ADV-01 My advisor & degree plan. The first checklist item reads the advisor assignment. If no advisor has been assigned yet, this item states that plainly and names the department office instead of showing an empty screen.
  • STU-TIME-01 Timetable. The second checklist item opens the real weekly timetable. If registration is not complete, it shows the programme's provisional schedule with a note that it will firm up after registration.
  • STU-PAY-01 Fee dashboard. The third checklist item opens the fee dashboard and pulls the earliest due date and amount forward into the finish summary.
  • STU-LEARN-01 Live class join. The fourth item shows what a live class card looks like and what happens on tapping join, so the first real class is not the first time she sees it.
  • STU-MOB-01 Mobile app. The notification permission granted here is what makes every later reminder possible; a student who declines is offered the choice again from settings and once more when her first payment falls due.
  • STU-HOME-01 Home cockpit. Completion state travels to the home screen: an unfinished checklist appears there as a chip for thirty days, then quietly stops.

Rules in plain words

  • Sign-in is always through the university's own system. The platform never creates a separate password for a student and never asks for the university password on its own screen.
  • One university email address maps to exactly one student record. If two records claim the same address, nobody is signed in and the case goes to registry — signing in the wrong student is worse than signing in nobody.
  • Name, date of birth, student number, programme and department are read-only to the student. She may request a correction; she may not make one.
  • Mobile number, alternate email, emergency contact and present address belong to the student and may be edited by her at any time, not only during orientation.
  • The identity card is a view of the record, never a separately stored fact. If registry changes her programme, the card shows the new programme the next time it refreshes.
  • An identity card is valid only while enrolment is active for the current semester. A withdrawn, suspended or graduated student sees a clearly marked expired or inactive card, never a valid-looking one.
  • The saved offline copy of the card carries the date it was last refreshed, so a guard can see whether it is stale.
  • Declining notifications never blocks orientation and never blocks any feature. It only changes how the student learns about things.
  • Orientation may be skipped and resumed. It is never a wall in front of a student who urgently needs to pay a fee or join a class already in progress.
  • A photograph uploaded by the student is a proposal, not a fact. Until registry approves it, the card shows a placeholder.

What can go wrong and what happens

  • The university email exists but no student record matches it. Common in the first week when admissions and registry are a few days apart. The student sees a plain message: her sign-in worked, but the university has not yet activated her student record, with the registry office name, room and phone number. No blank dashboard, no technical error text.
  • Two student records share one email address. Nobody is signed in. Both records are flagged for registry with a duplicate warning. The student sees a message asking her to visit registry with her admission letter.
  • The student never received a university email address. The "I do not know my university email" link explains where it is issued, what it usually looks like, and which desk to visit, and offers a callback request.
  • The university sign-in service is unreachable. The student sees that the university's sign-in is temporarily unavailable, not that the app is broken, with a retry button. Already signed-in students are unaffected.
  • The photograph upload fails on weak data. The upload resumes rather than restarting, and orientation continues; the photograph step can be completed later from the profile screen.
  • Enrolment is not yet active because admission fees are unpaid. Orientation still runs, so she can see her fee dashboard and pay, but the identity card shows "Not yet active" with the reason and the office named.
  • She abandons orientation halfway. Everything already done is kept. The home screen shows the remaining items. Nothing is lost and nothing is repeated.
  • She signs in on a second device. Orientation does not run again; the card and profile appear immediately. The saved offline card is written on each device separately.
  • Her account is disabled mid-session after a withdrawal. The next action she takes ends the session with a message naming registry. The saved offline card no longer refreshes and displays as expired after its validity date.

We know it works when

  • She has confirmed her identity, seen her advisor, timetable and first payment date, and holds a digital identity card that opens without a network — checked when she signs in for the first time and finishes orientation under: an admitted student with an active record and a university email address.
  • She sees a plain message naming the registry office and is not left on an empty dashboard — checked when she is returned to the app under: a student whose university sign-in succeeds but who has no matching student record.
  • No session is created, both records are flagged for registry, and the student is told to visit registry with her admission letter — checked when either sign-in is attempted under: two student records claiming the same university email address.
  • The fields are not editable and a correction request is offered instead — checked when she tries to change her name or student number under: a student viewing her welcome screen.
  • The completed items remain ticked and the checklist resumes from the third item — checked when she returns the next day under: a student who leaves orientation after two of five items.
  • The card is clearly marked as not yet active with the reason and the responsible office, and never resembles a valid card — checked when she opens her identity card under: a student whose enrolment is not yet active.
  • Every feature still works and she is offered the choice again from settings — checked when she completes orientation under: a student who declined notifications.
  • It displays within two seconds and shows the date it was last refreshed — checked when the card is opened at the campus gate under: a saved identity card on a phone with no network.

STU-HOME-01 · Home cockpit

P0

In one line. One screen that answers "what do I have to do next, and what is stopping me" in a single glance, using a small set of ranked chips. As a student with three free minutes between classes, I want to open the app and immediately see the one thing that matters most right now.

What this feature does

The home cockpit is the screen a student opens dozens of times a week and reads for about four seconds each time. Every other feature in this module is a destination; this is the junction. It exists because a university term generates a stream of unrelated obligations from unrelated offices — a class starting now, a mark just posted, an instalment due Thursday, an advising slot on Sunday, a library hold placed this morning — and no student should have to visit five screens to discover which of them is urgent.

It is used by every student, every day, from the first week to the last, and it is used most heavily on a phone in transit. It replaces the two habits that a bad portal creates: checking nothing until a crisis, and checking everything anxiously. A good cockpit is trusted enough that a student who sees nothing urgent believes it and closes the app.

The core design idea is ranking, not listing. The screen shows a small number of chips — compact cards, each with a title, one line of detail and one action — ordered strictly by how much damage ignoring them would do. A live class starting in four minutes beats a blocking hold, because the class is gone forever in ten minutes and the hold will still be there tonight. A blocking hold beats a payment due, because the hold is already preventing something. A payment due beats an advising appointment. An advising appointment beats work due in three days. Only when nothing is urgent does the screen relax into a calm summary of the day.

The cockpit invents nothing. Each chip is a faithful reading of a record another feature or module owns, and tapping a chip always lands on the real screen where the thing can actually be dealt with. If the fee dashboard says nothing is owed, no payment chip appears — the cockpit does not estimate, predict or prompt on amounts it cannot see.

What the student sees

Greeting strip

Her first name, today's date, the current semester and week number — for example "Spring 2026 · Week 7". Small, quiet, and useful because midterm week is a real anchor in a student's head.

Appears only while a class she is registered for is actually running or starts within fifteen minutes. How it works, step by step

  1. 1
    StudentOpens the app or the home page.
  2. 2
    SystemAsks each source for the student's current position: is a class running, are there holds, what is owed and when, is an advising appointment booked, what work is due, what has been posted since her last visit.
  3. 3
    SystemBuilds only the chips that have a real reason to exist. A source with nothing to say produces no chip; a source that cannot be reached produces a small honest note rather than an empty space.
  4. 4

  5. 5

  6. 6
    StudentReads the top chip and either acts on it or closes the app.
  7. 7
    StudentTaps a chip — for example the payment chip.
  8. 8

  9. 9

  10. 10
    SystemOn return, rebuilds the cockpit so the resolved chip is gone. A chip that is still there after a completed action is treated as a defect, not a refresh delay.
  11. 11
    SystemWhile the app is open, refreshes quietly on a short cycle so a class that becomes live appears without the student reloading.

What it connects with

  • STU-LEARN-01 Live class join. The live chip reads the running or imminent session for any course she is registered in and hands the join action straight to that feature, including its audio-only choice.
  • STU-REG-02 Hold centre. The blocking hold chip shows only holds that are actively preventing something. The hold centre supplies the hold's name, the owning office and what it blocks; the cockpit adds no interpretation of its own.
  • STU-PAY-01 Fee dashboard and STU-PAY-03 Installments. The payment chip reads the nearest unpaid due amount and date. If an instalment plan is active, the plan's next date wins over the overall balance, because that is the date with a consequence.
  • STU-ADV-02 Book advising appointment. A booking within seven days becomes a chip; an open advising period with no booking becomes a gentler prompt with the closing date.
  • STU-ASMT-01 Assignments & quizzes. Work-due chips read the due dates and the student's own submission state, so a submitted assignment stops appearing immediately.
  • STU-ASMT-02 Grades & standing. "Recently posted" reads newly posted marks — and only posted ones, so a mark a teacher is still drafting never leaks onto the home screen.
  • STU-TIME-01 Timetable. Today's classes strip and the week number both come from the timetable and the academic calendar, including makeup classes and cancellations.
  • STU-EXAM-01 Exam information. During examination weeks the exam schedule replaces ordinary class entries in today's strip, and an admit card that is available or blocked is surfaced as a chip.
  • STU-ONB-01 First login. An unfinished orientation checklist appears as a low-priority chip for thirty days after first sign-in.
  • STU-MOB-01 Mobile app. Every chip has a matching notification, and tapping that notification opens the same destination the chip would have opened.

Rules in plain words

  • Chips are ranked by consequence, never by which system answered first: live class, then blocking hold, then payment due, then advising, then work due.
  • A chip exists only when there is something real behind it. The cockpit never shows a placeholder, a sample or an encouraging message pretending to be a task.
  • Every chip is tappable and every tap lands on the exact item, not on the top of a long list.
  • No more than three work-due chips are shown at once; the rest live behind one link, so the screen cannot become a wall.
  • The live chip appears fifteen minutes before the scheduled start and disappears when the session ends, using the university's clock rather than the phone's.
  • Only holds that are currently blocking something get a chip. Informational flags stay in the hold centre.
  • Marks appear in "recently posted" only after the teacher has posted them. There is no earlier signal — not a count, not a hint, not a "your teacher is grading" note.
  • If a source cannot be reached, its area says so plainly. The screen never silently omits a payment that might exist.
  • Offline, the last known state is shown with the time it was captured.
  • Completing an action removes its chip on return. A stale chip is a bug.

What can go wrong and what happens

  • Everything is urgent at once. Live class, hold, payment and three assignments together. The order holds firm and only the top four chips are shown, with a "see everything" link below. Cramming eight chips into a phone screen makes all of them invisible.
  • The finance source is unavailable. The payment area shows that fee information could not be loaded and offers retry. It never renders as "nothing owed", because a student who trusts that and misses a deadline has been actively harmed.
  • A class is cancelled after the live chip appears. On the next quiet refresh the chip is replaced by a cancellation note that stays for the rest of the scheduled slot, so a student who is already walking to the room finds out.
  • The phone clock is wrong. All countdowns come from the university's clock. A phone set two hours fast never shows a class as missed when it has not started.
  • A hold is cleared while she is looking at the screen. The chip disappears on the next refresh, and if she taps it in the meantime the hold centre tells her plainly that it has just been cleared.
  • She has no registered courses yet. Instead of an empty screen, the cockpit explains where she is in the term — advising open, registration pending — and points at the next real step.
  • She is on leave or her semester has ended. The cockpit says so and shows only what still applies: outstanding dues, documents, results.
  • Extremely slow connection. The greeting, today's classes and any cached chips appear first; live sources fill in as they answer. The screen is never blank while waiting on the slowest source.
  • Two devices open at once. Both show the same state after a refresh; neither holds a private version of the truth.

We know it works when

  • The live class chip is first and the payment chip is below it — checked when the student opens the home screen under: a class starting in four minutes and an unpaid instalment due in three days.
  • The hold chip is first, names the Accounts office, and states exactly what it is preventing — checked when the home screen loads with no live class under: a blocking finance hold.
  • She sees a plain statement that nothing needs attention, plus her next class and next payment date, and no invented tasks — checked when she opens the home screen under: a student with nothing urgent.
  • The payment chip is gone and any hold that payment released is gone with it — checked when she completes the payment and returns under: a payment chip that the student taps.
  • The payment area states that fee information could not be loaded and offers retry, and never implies that nothing is owed — checked when the home screen loads under: the finance source is unreachable.
  • At most three work chips are shown, ordered by nearest deadline, with a link to the rest — checked when the home screen loads under: five assignments due this week.
  • Nothing about that mark appears anywhere on it — checked when the student opens the home screen under: a teacher who has graded but not posted a mark.
  • The last known chips are shown together with the time they were captured and a refresh control — checked when the home screen opens under: a phone with no network.
  • The countdown follows the university's clock and the class is not shown as missed — checked when the live chip is displayed under: a phone clock set two hours ahead.

Learning

Four features carry the teaching week: getting into the room, catching up on what was missed, finding the material, and knowing where to be next.

STU-LEARN-01 · Live class join

P0

In one line. One tap gets the student into the class, attendance is recorded the moment she arrives, an audio-only path keeps her in the room on weak internet, and a dropped connection reconnects her without losing her place. As a student on a two megabit connection, I want joining class to be one tap and I want to still be counted present when my video cannot cope.

What this feature does

This is the feature students touch most often and forgive least. A live class in a Bangladeshi private university is either fully online, fully in a room with an online section for students who could not travel, or a hybrid where the teacher streams from the classroom. In all three shapes the student's need is identical: get in, be counted, hear the teacher. Everything else — chat, hand raising, screen sharing — is secondary to those three.

The feature is used from the first teaching week to the last, several times a day, overwhelmingly on a phone. The student may be at home on shared broadband, on mobile data in a bus, or on campus wireless shared by hundreds of people. Load-shedding, monsoon weather and cell congestion are ordinary conditions, not exceptions to be handled later. A join flow that assumes stable bandwidth will fail for a large fraction of the class every single day.

Two problems are solved here. The first is friction: a student should never be hunting for a meeting link in an email, a chat group or a notice board. The class card is in the app, on the home screen, in the timetable and inside the course, and every one of them is the same single tap. The second is fairness in attendance. Attendance at these universities has real consequences — many require a minimum attendance percentage to sit the final examination — so recording it must be automatic, honest and visible to the student immediately, not a private note the teacher makes later that she discovers only when she is declared ineligible.

The audio-only path deserves particular emphasis because it is the difference between attending and not attending for many students. When bandwidth collapses, the feature does not drop her out; it drops the video and keeps her in the room, still counted present, still hearing the lecture, still able to see the shared slides as still images that update slowly. That is a deliberate ranking of what matters: presence and voice above picture quality.

What the student sees

What it connects with

  • Faculty module — live hosting. The teacher's session is the source of the class. Its start, end, recording setting, chat setting and late tolerance all come from there; this feature never starts a class on its own.
  • STU-TIME-01 Timetable. The scheduled slot, the room and any makeup or cancellation come from the timetable, so a rescheduled class is joinable at its new time and not at its old one.
  • STU-HOME-01 Home cockpit. The live chip is this feature's front door and carries the same join action.
  • STU-ASMT-02 Grades & standing. Attendance minutes recorded here feed the attendance percentage the student sees beside her marks, which is the same number used for examination eligibility.
  • STU-EXAM-01 Exam information. Where the university sets a minimum attendance to sit an examination, the eligibility statement on the exam screen is computed from the record this feature writes.
  • STU-LEARN-02 Recorded lecture playback. If the teacher recorded and published, the recording appears on the same class card, which is where a student who missed the class will look first.
  • STU-LEARN-03 Course workspace. Slides shared during class become material in the course when the teacher publishes them; the class card links there.
  • STU-MOB-01 Mobile app. The ten-minute reminder, the audio-only behaviour and the reconnection tolerance are all part of the phone-first promise.

Rules in plain words

  • Only students registered in that section may join. There is no shareable link that lets an unregistered person in.
  • Joining is possible from fifteen minutes before the scheduled start until the teacher ends the class.
  • Attendance is recorded at the moment of joining and confirmed on screen immediately. A student always knows her own attendance state.
  • All timing uses the university's clock. A wrong phone clock never affects whether a student is marked late.
  • What counts as present, late or absent is the university's rule, not the platform's guess, and the rule in force is shown to the student on the class card.
  • Short disconnections within the university's tolerance are one continuous attendance, not two visits and a gap.
  • When bandwidth falls, video is sacrificed before audio, and audio before presence. Being removed from the class is the last resort, never the first response.
  • Audio-only attendance counts exactly the same as video attendance. A student is never penalised for a weak connection.
  • A student may never edit her own attendance. Only the teacher may correct it, and every correction carries a reason that the student can read.
  • Recording is the teacher's decision. If the class is being recorded, students are told at the moment they join.

What can go wrong and what happens

  • The teacher never starts the class. After a grace period past the scheduled start, the card states that the class has not started and offers a way to notify the department, rather than leaving students refreshing for an hour.
  • The student joins forty minutes late. She enters normally, her true arrival time is recorded, and the card shows how the university's rule treats it. Nothing is hidden and nothing is softened.
  • Her connection dies for fifteen minutes. Reconnection is attempted throughout. If she returns within the tolerance, attendance is continuous; if not, the true minutes present are recorded and she can see them and ask the teacher for a correction.
  • Two devices join at once. One attendance record exists for the meeting. Overlapping time is counted once, so a second device cannot inflate minutes.
  • Her microphone or camera is blocked by the phone. She still joins and still listens; a small note explains how to grant permission. Permission problems never block entry.
  • The class runs beyond its scheduled end. Attendance keeps counting until the teacher ends it. A student who leaves at the scheduled end is not marked short for the overrun.
  • She is dropped from the course after attending. Past attendance is preserved as history; she simply cannot join future meetings.
  • The class is cancelled while she is waiting. The card becomes a cancellation notice with any note the teacher left, and no absence is recorded for anybody.
  • She has a blocking hold. Learning is not blocked by holds. She joins class normally; holds block registration, examinations and documents, never attending a class she is registered for.
  • Extremely low bandwidth from the start. Audio-only is preselected on the pre-join screen with an explanation, so her first experience is a working class rather than a frozen screen.

  • Audio-only is preselected with an explanation and her attendance counts exactly as a video join would — checked when she joins under: a connection too weak for video.
  • She returns to the same class and her attendance is one continuous record — checked when she reconnects under: a student whose connection drops for two minutes during class.
  • She is refused with a plain explanation and no attendance record is created — checked when she attempts to join under: a student not registered in the section.
  • She is not marked late — checked when she joins on time by the university clock under: a phone clock set thirty minutes fast.
  • She sees the corrected state and the reason the teacher gave — checked when the student views the class card under: a teacher who corrects an attendance entry.
  • Overlapping minutes are counted once — checked when attendance is calculated under: the same student joining from a phone and a laptop simultaneously.
  • She is told at that moment that the class is being recorded — checked when the student joins under: a class the teacher chose to record.

STU-LEARN-02 · Recorded lecture playback

P0

In one line. Watch what was missed or re-watch what was hard, with the position remembered across devices, a searchable transcript, and a low-data mode that respects a metered connection. As a student who missed Tuesday's class because of a transport strike, I want to catch up tonight without spending half my data package.

What this feature does

Recorded lectures are the safety net under the whole teaching week. Students miss classes for reasons that are not moral failures: transport strikes, flooding, illness, a family obligation, an overlapping laboratory, or simply a connection that failed during the live session. Without a reliable recording, one missed class becomes a permanent gap that shows up in the midterm. With one, the term stays recoverable.

The feature is also used heavily by students who did attend. Revision before a midterm or final is the second peak of usage, and it behaves differently: the student is not watching a lecture end to end, she is hunting for the eleven minutes where the teacher explained one confusing thing. That is why the transcript matters as much as the video. Being able to search the spoken words of a ninety-minute lecture and jump to the right moment turns a recording from an archive into a study tool.

Data cost is a first-class design concern, not a setting buried three levels deep. A student on a metered package will simply not watch if she believes a lecture costs her a large slice of her monthly data. The feature therefore states the approximate cost before playing, offers a genuinely low-data mode, and offers the transcript as an alternative to video altogether — reading a lecture costs almost nothing and is sometimes the better study method anyway.

Ownership is strict: the teacher decides whether a class is recorded, whether that recording is published, and how long students may see it. The module never publishes on the teacher's behalf and never keeps a recording visible past the teacher's or the university's retention window.

Rules in plain words

  • Only students registered in the section may view its recordings, and only while they remain registered.
  • A recording is visible only after the teacher publishes it. An unpublished recording does not exist as far as the student is concerned — no title, no duration, no hint.
  • Watching a recording never counts as attendance for that class.
  • The approximate data cost is shown before playback begins, every time, not once at first use.
  • Low-data mode must be genuinely small enough to be usable on a metered package; a slightly smaller video does not satisfy this rule.
  • Playback position is remembered per student per recording and follows her across devices.
  • Machine-made transcripts are always labelled as machine-made.
  • Downloading for offline viewing is available only where the university permits it, and a downloaded copy expires with the viewing window.
  • Recordings disappear when the viewing window or retention period ends, and the remaining time is stated in advance so a student can plan revision.
  • Teachers see watch statistics for the section as aggregates; no student's viewing is displayed to classmates.

What can go wrong and what happens

  • The recording is still being prepared. The entry says it is being prepared and gives an expected time, instead of a broken player.
  • The teacher never publishes. The class card states that no recording is available and points to the materials and the discussion thread.
  • Playback stalls repeatedly. The player offers to drop to low data or switch to the transcript, rather than buffering silently.
  • The transcript is wrong in places. The label warns her, and a "report a problem" link on the transcript tells the teacher, who can correct it.
  • The viewing window closes while she is watching. The current session finishes; the recording is not available the next time she opens it, and the entry explains that the window has closed.
  • She drops the course. Access ends with registration. Her saved positions are kept in case she re-registers later.

STU-LEARN-03 · Course workspace

P0

In one line. The single home of one course: the syllabus, the week-by-week modules and topics, every material the teacher published, the announcements, and the discussion where questions get answered. As a student, I want one place per course where I can find anything the teacher has given us, instead of hunting through a messaging group.

What this feature does

A student takes four to six courses a term. Each one has its own teacher, its own rhythm, its own pile of slides, readings and problem sets, and its own stream of announcements. Without a workspace, all of that ends up scattered across a messaging group, a shared drive folder somebody's classmate created, printed handouts and a notice board. Material gets lost, the wrong version circulates, and the student who was absent the day a link was shared never recovers.

The course workspace is the antidote: one address per course, organised the way the teacher teaches it. If the teacher structures the course in weeks, the workspace shows weeks; if in topics or chapters, it shows those. Inside each unit sit the things that belong there — the recording of the class, the slides, the reading, the assignment, the quiz — in the order the teacher intends. This is deliberate: a flat list of forty files sorted by upload date is not a course, and students revising for a final need the structure the teaching followed.

It is used constantly, in three distinct modes. During the teaching week it is a lookup tool: what is this week's reading, where is the assignment brief. Before an assessment it becomes a revision surface: everything from week one to week six in order. Between classes it is a communication channel: the announcement the teacher posted about a room change, the discussion thread where three classmates already asked the question she is about to ask.

The workspace shows, it does not author. Every material, announcement and structural decision belongs to the teacher in the Faculty module. The student's own contributions are her discussion posts and her submissions, and those flow outward to the teacher's queues. The workspace also never reveals a mark; a graded assignment shows here as submitted, and its result lives with grades where posting rules apply.

Rules in plain words

  • Only students registered in the section see the workspace, and access begins when the teacher opens the course.
  • A material is visible only after its release time, which follows the university's clock.

STU-TIME-01 · Timetable & academic calendar

P0

In one line. Where the student has to be: a weekly class grid, the examination schedule, university holidays and events, and makeup classes folded in so the week she sees is the week that will actually happen. As a student, I want one truthful weekly view, including the makeup class my teacher added on Thursday.

What this feature does

A private-university week in Bangladesh is not a clean grid. Classes are spread across Sunday to Thursday with laboratories in long evening blocks, rooms change at short notice, national and religious holidays move by a day depending on the moon, teachers schedule makeup classes for anything missed, and the examination weeks overwrite the ordinary pattern entirely. A student who relies on the schedule she memorised in week one will be in the wrong room by week seven.

This feature is the answer to "where do I have to be", at three zoom levels. Today, for the student walking across campus. This week, for planning around a part-time job or the tuition she gives. This term, for knowing when midterms start, when the drop deadline falls, when the campus is closed, and when finals end. It is used every day, and its whole value rests on being correct — a timetable that is right most of the time is worse than useless, because the student stops checking it.

It also makes the academic calendar visible in the same place as classes, which most portals fail to do. Deadlines that are not classes — the last day to drop a course, the fee instalment date, the course evaluation window, the results publication date — are exactly the dates students miss. Putting them on the same calendar as classes, with the same reminders, prevents an entire category of expensive mistakes.

Like the rest of the module, it authors nothing. Class slots come from the registered sections, changes and makeups come from teachers and the department, and the academic calendar comes from the registrar. The feature's craft is in merging these sources into one week without contradictions, and in being explicit whenever something has changed.

STU-ASMT-01 · Assignment & quiz taking

P0

In one line. Handing in work without fear: file, typed text or timed quiz, uploads that survive a dropped connection, attempt rules stated before she starts, and a receipt that proves the work arrived. As a student uploading at 11:52 pm on a shaky connection, I want to know for certain whether my work is in.

What this feature does

Submitting work is the highest-anxiety moment in a student's week, and the anxiety is rational. The consequences of a failed submission are severe and irreversible: a zero on a component worth ten or fifteen per cent of a course grade. In Bangladesh the failure modes are ordinary rather than exotic — the electricity goes at 11:40 pm, the phone's data allowance runs out mid-upload, the file is a forty megabyte scan of handwritten pages photographed one by one, or the browser is closed by an incoming call during a timed quiz.

This feature therefore has one guiding principle: never lose a student's work, and never leave her uncertain about whether it arrived. Everything else follows. Uploads resume from where they stopped instead of restarting. Typed answers are saved continuously as she writes, not when she presses submit. A quiz in progress survives a closed app and reopens where it was, with the clock respected honestly. Every completed submission produces a receipt she can save and show.

The second job is clarity before commitment. A student should know, before she begins, what kind of submission this is, how many attempts she has, whether the clock starts when she opens it, what happens if she is late, and what file types and sizes are accepted. The overwhelming majority of submission disputes at a university registrar's desk come from a rule that existed but was never shown at the moment it mattered.

The feature covers three shapes of work. File submissions — reports, laboratory sheets, scanned handwriting, code archives, slide decks. Text submissions — written directly in the app for short answers and reflections. Quizzes — a set of questions with a time limit, delivered one at a time or all together as the teacher chose. Each shape has different failure modes, and each gets a matching safety net.

  • A quiz clock starts when the student starts and continues in real time; closing the app does not pause it, and the start screen says so.
  • Answers are saved as she moves through a quiz, so a disconnection loses at most the current question.
  • Where the teacher allows multiple attempts, which attempt counts — highest, latest or average — is stated before the first attempt.
  • Late work is accepted only if the teacher allowed it, and the penalty is shown before she submits late, not applied silently afterwards.
  • A student may never see a mark or feedback before the teacher posts it, even if grading is finished.
  • A submitted file may be replaced only while attempts remain and the window is open; every version is kept, and the teacher sees which one is current.

What can go wrong and what happens

  • The connection dies at ninety per cent. The upload resumes when the network returns. If the deadline passes while it is still incomplete, no submission exists, and the screen is honest about that rather than showing a spinner and a false hope.
  • She submits at 11:59:40 pm and it completes at 12:00:12 am. The receipt records the true completion time and marks it late under the teacher's policy. The rule is applied consistently, and she can see both times.
  • The file is larger than allowed. She is told before the upload begins, with the limit and the actual size, and offered compression guidance for photographed pages.
  • The file type is not accepted. Refused immediately at selection with the accepted list, never after a long upload.
  • Her phone dies mid-quiz. Answers already given are saved. Reopening returns her to the quiz with the remaining time honestly reduced by the elapsed real time.
  • The quiz window closes while she is answering. Everything answered so far is submitted automatically at the closing moment, and she is shown what was captured.
  • She uploads the wrong file and notices after submitting. If attempts remain and the window is open, she may replace it and the receipt is reissued. If not, the card tells her to contact the teacher.
  • The teacher extends the deadline after some students submitted. Earlier submissions remain valid, the new deadline is shown to everyone, and previously late submissions are re-evaluated against the new date.
  • Two devices submit at once. One submission wins and the other is refused with a clear message; there is never a silent duplicate in the teacher's queue.
  • A group assignment. One member submits on behalf of the group; every member sees the same receipt with the submitter's name on it, so nobody is left wondering whether it was done.
  • She is on academic probation and worried. Nothing about her standing changes the submission mechanics; the feature never treats one student differently from another.

  • She receives a receipt showing the exact university-clock time and the words "on time" — checked when the student completes her upload before it under: an assignment with a stated deadline.
  • The upload continues from seventy per cent and never restarts — checked when the network returns under: a connection that drops at seventy per cent of an upload.
  • The submission is recorded as late according to the university's clock — checked when the student submits five minutes after the true deadline under: a phone clock set ten minutes behind.
  • Reopening restores the text as it was — checked when the app is closed without submitting under: a typed answer in progress.
  • Her earlier answers are intact and ten minutes remain — checked when the student's phone dies at 2:10 pm and she returns at 2:20 pm under: a thirty-minute quiz started at 2:00 pm.
  • She is warned that no further attempt is possible before it is accepted — checked when she taps submit under: a single-attempt assessment.
  • It shows only that the work was submitted, with no mark and no hint of one — checked when the student opens the assessment card under: a teacher who has graded but not posted.
  • It is refused immediately with the accepted list shown, before any upload begins — checked when the student selects it under: a file type the teacher did not allow.
  • The student is told clearly whether a submission exists and how it was treated — checked when the upload finally finishes under: a deadline that passes during an incomplete upload.

STU-ASMT-02 · Grades & standing

P0

In one line. Every mark the teacher has posted, with its weight, the running total it produces, the grade point average across the degree, and her attendance standing — with nothing shown before the teacher posts it. As a student, I want to know exactly where I stand and how the number was arrived at.

What this feature does

Marks are the most emotionally charged data in the whole product, and the least well handled by traditional university systems. The usual experience is a printed list on a departmental notice board, a number with no breakdown, and a rumour mill about when results will appear. Students respond by asking the teacher directly, by asking classmates, and by refreshing a portal a hundred times. This feature exists to replace all of that with one honest, always-current screen.

It runs through the entire term, not only at the end. Continuous assessment means marks arrive one at a time: quiz one in week three, assignment one in week four, the midterm in week eight. Each posted mark changes her running position, and seeing that position early is what allows a student to act while acting is still possible. A student who discovers in week twelve that she needed better quiz marks has been failed by her tools.

The screen shows three layers. The component layer: each assessed piece with its marks, the maximum, the weight and the teacher's feedback. The course layer: the running total across posted components, the grade so far, and attendance standing against the university's requirement. The degree layer: grade point average for the term and cumulative, credits earned, and academic standing in plain words — good standing, warning, probation — with what that means and what happens next.

The strictest rule in this module lives here. A mark is visible only after the teacher posts it. Not when grading begins, not when grading finishes, not when the department reviews. There is no progress bar showing how much of the class has been marked, no count of graded scripts, no notification that grading has started. Between submission and posting, the student sees "submitted" and nothing more.

What the student sees

Overview

One card per registered course: code, title, credit hours, the running total across posted components, the attendance percentage, and a one-word state. A quiet note reminds her the total covers posted components only.

Your total reflects only the marks your teachers have posted so far. Components not yet posted are not included.
Inside one course

The assessment breakdown as a table: component name, weight, marks obtained out of the maximum, and the date it was posted. Components not yet posted appear by name and weight with the marks column showing that it is not posted, so the shape of the course is visible without any leak.

Quiz 1 · 5% · 8 out of 10 · posted 12 March · Quiz 2 · 5% · not posted yet · Midterm · 25% · not posted yet
How the total was calculated

An expandable explanation in words, using her actual numbers, so the arithmetic is never a mystery. This single element removes most of the mark queries a department receives.

You have 8 out of 10 in Quiz 1, which is worth 5%. That gives you 4 out of the 5 marks available. Across all posted components you have 31.5 out of the 40 marks posted so far.
Feedback

The teacher's written comments and any annotated file, attached to the component they belong to, opened from the same row as the mark.

Attendance standing

Classes held, classes attended, percentage, and the university's requirement stated beside it, with a clear statement of consequence when she is near or below the line.

You have attended 18 of 24 classes (75%). Your university requires 70% to sit the final examination. You may miss at most 2 more classes.
Final grade

Once the teacher posts the final grade and the department certifies it, the letter grade and grade point appear with the date. Until then the course shows a running total and a plain statement that the final grade is not published.

Degree standing

Term grade point average, cumulative grade point average, credits earned against credits required, and academic standing in words with its consequence.

Cumulative grade point average 3.42 · 78 of 130 credits earned · Good standing
Probation state

Where standing is a warning or probation, the screen states it plainly, explains what the university requires to return to good standing, and offers a direct route to book advising.

You are on academic probation. Your university requires a term grade point average of 2.00 or above next term to return to good standing. Talk to your advisor before registering.
Blocked by evaluation

Where the university's policy withholds results until the course evaluation is done, the course shows that reason and a direct link to complete it — never a blank space or a vague error.

Your results for this course are held until you complete the course evaluation. It takes about two minutes and your answers are anonymous.
Past terms

Every previous term with its courses, grades, credits and term average, which is what students use for scholarship applications and job forms — and which is the informal view, with the official transcript available through documents.

  • A course is dropped mid-term. It appears with the withdrawal notation and is excluded from the term average according to the university's rule.

STU-EXAM-01 · Exam information

P1

In one line. Everything about sitting an examination: the midterm and final schedule, the seat and room, whether she is eligible, and the admit card that becomes downloadable once fees are clear. As a student three days before my final, I want to know whether I can sit it and to hold my admit card on my phone.

What this feature does

Examinations carry most of the grade at a Bangladeshi private university — a midterm and a final together often account for sixty per cent or more. Around them sits a set of administrative conditions that students routinely discover too late: a minimum attendance percentage, a requirement that all dues are cleared, a signed admit card that invigilators check at the hall door, and a seat plan published only days in advance.

This feature brings all of that into one screen, ahead of time. Its most valuable function is not the schedule; it is the eligibility statement. Every student, weeks before the examination, can see whether anything currently stands between her and the hall, what it is, which office owns it, and how much time she has to fix it. That converts the most common catastrophe at these universities — being turned away at the examination hall — into a solvable problem discovered in week nine.

The admit card is the artefact. In practice it is a printed or digital card carrying the student's photograph, student number, programme, the examinations she is permitted to sit with their dates, times, rooms and seats, and the university's authorisation. It becomes available only when the conditions are met, and the screen is explicit about which condition is not yet met rather than simply refusing to produce it.

The feature is used in two bursts each term, before midterms and before finals, and heavily in the last week before each. Load is concentrated and the stakes are high, so the screen is deliberately simple and the download must work on a weak connection and remain readable offline.

What the student sees

Instructions

The university's examination rules in plain sentences: how early to arrive, what may be carried in, what is prohibited, what happens if she arrives late, and what to do if she falls ill.

Days remaining to the next examination, and reminders the day before and one hour before, each carrying room and seat.

Where the student has an approved accommodation — extra time, a separate room, a scribe — it is shown on the row and printed on the admit card, so no explanation is needed at the door.

Nothing scheduled

STU-PAY-01 · Fee dashboard

P0

In one line. Every Taka the university says she owes, in one honest list — tuition, retake, laboratory, hostel, library fines and the rest — with waivers and scholarships shown as reductions rather than hidden inside a net figure. What this feature does

Money is where a private university relationship is most often damaged, and almost always for the same reason: the student did not know. She did not know a retake carried its own fee, that the laboratory charge was separate from tuition, that a library fine had been added in March, or that her waiver applied to tuition but not to the development fee. The fee dashboard exists to make that impossible. It shows the complete financial position at all times, itemised, dated and explained. A net figure with no breakdown teaches a student nothing and is impossible to check.

It is consulted at predictable moments: at the start of term when charges appear, before each instalment date, before examinations when the admit card depends on it, and at the end of a degree during clearance. What this feature does

Three things make or break trust here. STU-PAY-05 · Bank slip / offline payment

P1

In one line. For everyone who cannot or will not pay online: a printable bank deposit slip with the right details already on it, a place to upload the stamped copy, and honest tracking of the accounts office verification. As a student whose father pays at a bank branch, I want the deposit to be matched to my fees without me queuing at the Accounts counter.

What this feature does

A significant share of university fees in Bangladesh is still paid at a bank counter. The reasons are practical rather than nostalgic: the amount exceeds a mobile wallet's transaction limit, the payer is a parent who trusts a bank and not an app, the family is sending a demand draft, or the university's arrangement with a particular bank gives it the cleanest reconciliation. A product that treats offline payment as a failure case will simply push those students to a physical counter for everything.

The feature does three things. It produces a correctly filled deposit slip so the bank teller has the university's account details, the student's identity and the exact amount, which prevents the most common cause of an unmatched deposit — a payment with nothing on it to say whose fees it is. It gives the student a way to upload the stamped slip immediately after paying, so the accounts office can verify without her travelling to campus. And it tracks verification honestly, telling her where the payment stands and what will happen next.

Honesty about timing is the heart of it. An offline payment does not clear instantly, and pretending otherwise creates the worst outcome: a student who believes her admit card will appear and discovers on the morning of the examination that it has not. Every screen states plainly that the payment counts only when the accounts office verifies it, and gives a realistic expectation of how long that takes.

The feature is used most heavily by first-year students whose families are paying the largest admission-time amounts, by evening and part-time students who work during office hours, and by anybody paying an amount above a wallet's limit.

What this feature does

A four-year degree at a private university in Bangladesh is typically a hundred and thirty or more credits arranged into general education, core, major, elective and capstone requirements, with prerequisites and a minimum grade in some courses. Students navigate this almost blind. The rules live in a catalogue nobody reads, the progress lives in a spreadsheet the department keeps, and the only reliable interpreter is an advisor who has ninety advisees and two weeks of advising period.

The result is well known on every campus: students who take courses in the wrong order, who discover in their final term that they are three credits short, who repeat an elective that did not count, or who lose a term because a prerequisite was missed.   • The student does not turn up. The advisor marks it, the slot is released, and the department can see repeated non-attendance — which matters when advising slots are scarce.
  • She books and then registration closes. The appointment stands but the screen warns that registration has closed and names who may grant a late registration.
  • The slot clashes with a class. She is warned before confirming and may proceed knowingly.
  • All slots are taken in advising week. The screen states when more are expected, and the asynchronous question route is offered so approval is not impossible.
  • She needs a different advisor for a specialised question. The screen explains that advisor assignment is the department's and offers the route to request a change.
  • The online meeting fails on a weak connection. The same audio-only path as a class applies, and if the meeting cannot happen the advisor can rebook without the student losing her place.
  • The advisor records an approval for the wrong courses. The student sees the recorded approval on her plan and can raise it before registering, which is why it is shown rather than applied invisibly.

We know it works when

  • Only those slots are offered, with time, length and whether each is in office or online — checked when her advisee opens booking under: an advisor who published slots.
  • Exactly one booking exists and the other student is offered alternatives immediately — checked when both submit under: two students confirming the same slot simultaneously.
  • She is warned about the clash before confirming — checked when the student selects it under: a slot that clashes with a registered class.
  • She has received a reminder the day before and an hour before, each carrying the place or the join control — checked when the day arrives under: a confirmed appointment.
  • Every affected student is notified with a reason and offered rebooking — checked when the cancellation is made under: an advisor who cancels a block.
  • The hold is released and registration becomes possible — checked when the advisor records approval at the meeting under: an advising hold requiring approval.
  • She is told when new slots are expected and offered the question route — checked when the student opens booking under: an advisor with no open slots.
  • A join control appears on the appointment fifteen minutes before — checked when the start time approaches under: an online appointment.

STU-ADV-03 · Ask my advisor

P2

In one line. A written question to the advisor that does not need a meeting, kept as a thread with a visible state, and routed to the right office instead when the matter is sensitive or belongs elsewhere. As a student with one question at 11 pm, I want to ask it properly and know it will be answered.

What this feature does

Most student questions do not need fifteen minutes of an advisor's time. "Does CSE 373 count towards my major elective?" needs two lines. Today those questions travel by personal mobile number, by messages sent to a teacher's private account at midnight, or by a student waiting outside an office for half an hour. All three are bad: they are invisible to the department, they are unanswerable when the teacher changes, and they place an unreasonable burden on the advisor's personal life.

This feature gives those questions a proper home. The student writes the question with her degree plan already attached as context, sends it, and sees its state. The advisor answers when she can, from the Faculty module, with the student's record beside the question. The thread persists so an answer given in March can be found again in October, which matters because the same student asks the same category of question every term.

The second and equally important job is routing. Some things a student writes to her advisor are not advising matters, and some are serious: financial hardship, harassment, mental health, medical emergency, a complaint about a teacher. An advisor is not always the right or the safe recipient, and a system that treats every message identically can leave a vulnerable student waiting for an answer that never comes. The feature therefore recognises the categories the university has defined as sensitive and offers the correct office directly, with the university's own guidance and, where appropriate, an immediate contact number rather than a queue.

Usage is steady rather than peaked — a few messages per student per term — but it carries disproportionate weight, because it is the channel through which a struggling student first says something.

What the student sees

Ask box

A topic chosen from what the department configured, a written question, and an optional attachment. Her degree plan and current registration are attached automatically as context, and she is told they will be.

Your advisor will see your degree plan and your current courses along with this question, so you do not need to explain them.
Expected response time

Stated honestly before sending, based on what the department publishes, so a student does not sit refreshing.

Advisors usually reply within two working days. During advising week it may take longer.
Thread

Question and replies in order with who wrote each and when, and a clear state: waiting for advisor, answered, or closed. The student may reply within the thread rather than starting a new question.

Sensitive topic routing

When she selects a topic the university has marked sensitive, the screen does not send it to the advisor. It names the right office, explains what that office does, and offers the direct route — including an immediate telephone number where the university has designated one.

This is handled by the Office of Student Welfare rather than your academic advisor. They can be reached confidentially on the number below, or you can send a private message that only their counsellors can read.
Urgent notice

Where the matter is one the university has defined as urgent, the screen says plainly that a written message is not the fastest route and gives the number to call now.

If you need help immediately, please call the number below rather than waiting for a reply here.
Wrong-office redirect

Where the advisor decides a question belongs to another office — a fee query to Accounts, a transcript question to the Registrar — she can redirect it, and the student sees where it went and who now owns it, rather than the thread going quiet.

Your advisor has passed this to the Accounts office. They now own your question and will reply here.
Answered

The reply with the date, and an option to say whether it resolved the matter, which is how a department learns which questions need a better answer in the handbook.

Escalation

Where no reply arrives within the published time, the student is offered a way to escalate to the department office, and the escalation is visible to both sides rather than being a complaint behind the advisor's back.

Past questions

Searchable, because a student who asked about an elective rule in March will need the same answer in October.

  2. 2

  3. 3

  5. 5

  9. 9

What it connects with

  • STU-ADV-01 My advisor & degree plan. Context travels with every question, so the advisor never has to ask which programme or which term.
  • STU-ADV-02 Book advising appointment. Offered when a question is too complex to answer in writing, and the thread links to the booking.
  • Faculty module — advisees. The advisor's queue lives there with the student's record attached.
  • Student welfare, counselling, registrar and accounts offices. Sensitive and misdirected topics are routed to the office the university designated, with its own contact route.
  • STU-PAY-01 Fee dashboard. A fee question redirected to Accounts carries the relevant charge rows as context.
  • STU-REG-02 Hold centre. A question about a hold links to the hold so the office answering can see exactly which one is meant.
  • Administrator module — department configuration. Topics, sensitivity, owning offices and response times are all configured there.

Rules in plain words

  • Every question has an owner and a visible state. A thread never goes silently unowned.
  • The expected response time is shown before the student sends, not after.
  • Sensitive topics defined by the university are never routed to the academic advisor by default.
  • Where the university has designated an urgent contact, it is offered instead of a written queue, plainly and without making the student search for it.
  • A redirect keeps the whole thread and tells the student who now owns it.
  • The student's own record is attached as context automatically; the advisor sees what she is already entitled to see and nothing more.
  • Threads persist and are searchable by the student.
  • Escalation after the published time is available and visible to both sides.
  • The channel is between the student and the university office; it never exposes an advisor's personal contact details.
  • A confidential route to a welfare office is not visible to the academic advisor.

What can go wrong and what happens

  • The advisor never replies. The published time passes, the student is offered escalation to the department office, and the unanswered thread is visible to the department.
  • The student writes something serious under an ordinary topic. The reply route to the welfare office is offered inside the thread, and the university's guidance is shown, so a misfiled message still reaches help.
  • She sends the same question five times. The threads are visible together and can be merged, so the advisor answers once.
  • The advisor changes mid-term. Open threads transfer to the new advisor with their history, and the student is told.
  • The question is really about a fee. The redirect moves it to Accounts with the charge rows attached, and the student sees the transfer.
  • She asks during advising week when reply times stretch. The expected time reflects the period rather than showing an optimistic figure she will lose faith in.
  • She attaches a large file on a weak connection. The upload resumes rather than restarting, exactly as in submissions.
  • The answer she receives is wrong. The thread is a record, which is precisely what allows a student to raise it with the department.
  • She graduates with a thread open. It is closed with a note naming the office to contact afterwards, rather than being abandoned.

We know it works when

  • It reaches her advisor with her degree plan and registration attached, and the expected reply time was shown before sending — checked when the student sends a question under: an ordinary advising topic.
  • The question is not routed to the academic advisor and the correct office is named with its contact route — checked when the student selects it under: a topic the university marked sensitive.
  • An immediate contact number is offered rather than a written queue — checked when the student selects it under: a matter the university defined as urgent.
  • The student sees the new owner and the full thread is preserved — checked when the redirect is made under: a question the advisor redirects to Accounts.
  • Escalation to the department office is offered and is visible to both sides — checked when the student opens the thread under: the published response time passes with no reply.
  • They transfer with their history and the student is told — checked when open threads exist under: an advisor change mid-term.
  • Its owner and state are always visible — checked when the student views it under: any thread.
  • It is not visible to her — checked when the academic advisor opens her queue under: a confidential welfare message.

STU-REG-01 · Course add/drop

P1

In one line. Adding or dropping a course inside the university's window, with the seat, the clash, the prerequisite and — above all — the exact money consequence shown before she confirms. Advisor-suggested courses from STU-ADV-01 appear first; adding or dropping updates that same degree-plan timeline. As a student thinking about dropping a course, I want to know what it will cost me before I tap the button, not afterwards.

What this feature does

Every term opens with a short window in which a student may adjust her registration: swap a section whose timing does not work, add a course a friend recommended, drop one that is too heavy alongside a job. The window is usually one to two weeks, and the rules attached to it are unforgiving. Drop in week one and the fee comes back; drop in week three and it does not; drop after the deadline and a withdrawal notation goes on the transcript.

The catastrophe this feature prevents is a student dropping a course without understanding the consequence. She drops in week three believing she will be refunded, discovers in week five that she owes the full amount, and now has a finance hold before her midterm. This is entirely preventable by showing the refund rule, in Taka, at the moment of decision — which is what this feature does and what almost no university portal does.

The second job is preventing invalid registrations before they happen: a course whose prerequisite she has not passed, a section already full, a clash with another registered class, a credit load above the permitted maximum, or an attempt to register while a hold is on her record. Each of these is checked and explained in words at the moment she tries, rather than surfacing later as a cancelled registration she was never told about.

The feature is only open when the university opens it. Outside the window it does not pretend to be available; it states when the window opens or closes and what the alternative route is. Where the university requires an advisor's approval before any change, that requirement is enforced here.

What the student sees

Window state

At the top, always: whether add and drop is open, and until when, on the university's clock. Where it is closed, when it opens or who may grant an exception.

Add and drop is open until Thursday 21 May, 11:59 pm.
Current registration

Every registered course with section, schedule, credits and the teacher, and a total credit load against the permitted range. A drop action on each row.

Registered: 5 courses · 15 credits · your programme allows 12 to 18 credits this term.
Finding a course to add

Search and filter by code, title, requirement group, day and time. Advisor-suggested courses from the degree plan appear first. Each result shows section, schedule, room, teacher, seats remaining, and — stated in words — whether she is eligible. Overrides the advisor cannot grant link to STU-ADV-04.

Suggested from your degree plan · CSE 373 Design and Analysis of Algorithms · Section 2 · Sun & Tue 1:00–2:30 · 4 seats left · You meet the prerequisite.
Why she cannot add

Where a course cannot be added, the specific reason in plain words rather than a greyed-out button: prerequisite not met, section full, clash with another class, credit limit reached, or a hold in place.

You cannot add this course. It clashes with MAT 120, which you are registered for on Sunday at 1:00 pm.
Adding — consequence first

Before confirming, the new charge in Taka, the new credit total, and the new weekly schedule with the added class in it.

Adding CSE 373 adds ৳24,000 to your fees for this term, due on your next instalment date. Your load becomes 18 credits.
Dropping — the refund consequence

The most important screen in the feature. Before confirming a drop: how much of the fee is returned under the university's rule for today's date, how much is not, whether a transcript notation applies, and what her load becomes. Stated in figures, not percentages alone.

Dropping CSE 373 today returns ৳12,000 of the ৳24,000 you were charged, because today is inside the 50% refund period, which ends on 21 May. After that date nothing is returned and a W appears on your transcript.
Below minimum load warning

Where a drop would take her below the minimum credits, a plain warning about what that affects — scholarship, hostel eligibility, student visa status for international students, or full-time standing.

Dropping this course takes you to 9 credits, below the 12 credit minimum for full-time status. This may affect your merit waiver. Talk to your advisor before confirming.
Advisor approval

Where the university requires it, the change is submitted for approval and shows as pending rather than done, with what happens next.

Your request to drop CSE 373 has been sent to Dr. Rahman for approval. It is not yet a drop — your registration is unchanged until she approves.
Confirmation

After the change: what was added or dropped, the new schedule, the new credit total, the fee change, and a link to the updated fee dashboard.

Blocked by a hold

Where a hold prevents registration entirely, the screen names it, names the office and links to the hold centre rather than showing a broken search.

  • STU-PAY-01 Fee dashboard. An add raises a charge and a drop produces a refund or credit, each appearing as its own line with the rule named.
  • STU-REG-02 Hold centre. A hold blocks registration entirely; the screen names it and links there, and clearing it makes registration possible immediately.
  • STU-TIME-01 Timetable. Clash checking reads the timetable, and a confirmed change updates the week at once.

Rules in plain words

  • Registration changes are possible only inside the university's window, judged on the university's clock.
  • The money consequence of a drop is always shown in Taka, for today's date, before the student confirms.
  • The refund schedule is the university's; the platform never estimates or rounds a refund of its own.
  • A reason for refusal is always named in words. A disabled button without an explanation is never acceptable.
  • Prerequisites, clashes, seat availability and credit limits are all checked before the change, not after.
  • A seat is taken only when the registration completes; two students cannot take the last seat.
  • Where advisor approval is required, the change is pending until approved and the screen says the registration is unchanged.
  • A drop that takes the student below a minimum load always carries a warning about what it affects.
  • Any transcript notation that will result from a drop is stated before the drop.
  • A hold that blocks registration blocks it entirely, and the owning office is always named.
  • Dropping never destroys past submissions, attendance or marks for the course.

What can go wrong and what happens

  • The last seat goes while she is deciding. The confirmation fails with a plain message, the course shows as full, and she is offered other sections of the same course.
  • She drops one day after the refund period ends. The screen shows zero refund and the date it ended before she confirms, so the decision is informed rather than discovered later.
  • Her prerequisite grade is under appeal. The course is not addable and the reason names the grade; the route to the department is offered, because only they can permit an exception.
  • A hold is placed while she is mid-change. The change is refused at confirmation with the hold named, rather than half-applied.
  • She adds a course that clashes with a makeup class scheduled later. The clash appears in the timetable when the makeup is published, with a warning, because a future makeup cannot be known at registration time.
  • The advisor rejects her drop request. The registration stands unchanged, and the advisor's reason is shown.
  • She drops all her courses. The screen warns that this amounts to withdrawing from the term, names the consequence and requires the university's withdrawal route rather than treating it as an ordinary drop.
  • The window closes while she has the screen open. The change is refused at confirmation with the closing time shown, rather than being accepted late.
  • She is an international student on a visa condition. The minimum load warning names the visa consequence where the university has configured it.
  • Finance has already invoiced the term. The change produces an adjustment line rather than editing the original invoice, so the trail stays auditable.

  • The closing date and time are shown at the top — checked when the student opens registration under: an open add and drop window.
  • She is refused with the specific prerequisite named in words — checked when she tries to add it under: a course whose prerequisite she has not passed.
  • The exact amount returned and the amount not returned are shown in Taka, with the date the period ends — checked when she opens the drop confirmation under: a drop inside a fifty per cent refund period.
  • She is told a withdrawal notation will appear on her transcript before she confirms — checked when she opens the confirmation under: a drop after the notation deadline.
  • She is warned what that affects and advised to speak to her advisor — checked when she opens the confirmation under: a drop that takes her below the full-time minimum.
  • Her timetable, course workspace and fee dashboard all reflect it immediately — checked when the registration completes under: a confirmed add.
  • The hold and its owning office are named and the hold centre is linked — checked when she opens registration under: a hold that blocks registration.
  • Only one registration succeeds and the other is told the section is full and offered alternatives — checked when both submit under: two students confirming the last seat at once.
  • It shows as pending and her registration is explicitly unchanged until the advisor approves — checked when the student submits a change under: a university that requires advisor approval.

STU-REG-02 · Hold centre

P0

In one line. Every hold on her record in one place — finance, advising, library, discipline — each naming the office that placed it, exactly what it is blocking, and precisely how it clears. As a student who cannot register and does not know why, I want one screen that tells me the truth.

What this feature does

A hold is a flag an office places on a student's record that prevents her from doing something until it is resolved. Finance places one for unpaid fees. The library places one for an unreturned book. The department places one when advising approval has not been given. Student discipline places one after a proceeding. Each office acts independently and, in most universities, invisibly — the student learns about a hold only when an action fails.

That invisibility is the problem this feature solves. The classic sequence at a Bangladeshi private university is: registration opens, the student tries to register, the system refuses without explanation, she goes to the department, they send her to Accounts, Accounts says it is the library, the library says the book was returned but the flag is still there, and four days of the registration window are gone. The hold centre collapses that into one screen: every hold, its owner, its reason, its consequence and its remedy.

The design rule that makes this trustworthy is that holds are rendered, never invented. The module does not decide that a student "probably" owes something, does not create a hold because a payment looks late, and does not keep showing one after the owning office has removed it. If the library clears the flag, the hold disappears on the next read. If finance places one at midnight, it appears immediately. The screen is a faithful mirror.

The feature is consulted at exactly the moments where holds bite: before registration, before examinations, when requesting a document, and during graduation clearance. It also pushes itself forward — a newly placed blocking hold produces a notification and a home screen chip, because a hold a student does not know about is the whole problem.

What the student sees

Clear state

When nothing is on her record, the screen says so plainly and lists what would have been blocked, so a student checking before registration gets a definite answer rather than an ambiguous empty page.

One card per hold: its type, the office that placed it, the date, the reason in the office's own words, what it blocks, and the action that clears it. Blocking holds come first; informational flags follow, visually distinct.

Finance hold

The amount that must be paid, what it is for, and a direct pay action. Where a partial payment would release it, that threshold is stated.

Finance hold · placed by the Accounts office on 15 May · Outstanding fees of ৳28,500 · Blocks: registration, admit card, transcript · Clears when: the outstanding amount is paid in full.

Advising hold · placed by the Department of Computer Science · Your advisor has not yet approved your courses for Summer 2026 · Blocks: registration · Clears when: Dr. Farhana Rahman records her approval.

Discipline hold

Stated on every card, in words, because two holds may block different things and a student needs to know which one is stopping the thing she is trying to do right now.

Pending clearance

Where she has acted but the office has not yet confirmed — a bank slip uploaded, a book returned this morning — the hold shows the pending action and honestly states that it remains until the office confirms.

Rules in plain words

STU-SRV-01 · Course evaluation survey

P1

In one line. The end-of-term evaluation of each course, genuinely anonymous, short enough to actually finish, and — where the university's policy says so — a gate that must be passed before results open. As a student, I want to say honestly what I thought of a course without any chance of it affecting my grade.

What this feature does

Universities collect course feedback for real reasons: teacher evaluation, promotion decisions, curriculum review, and accreditation requirements. Students supply it reluctantly, because the traditional version is a paper form handed out in the last class, filled in with the teacher standing at the front of the room, and no student in Bangladesh believes that is anonymous. The result is uniformly positive, useless feedback, and a widespread belief that speaking honestly is dangerous.

This feature is built around one non-negotiable commitment: the teacher can never learn who said what. The system knows whether a student completed the evaluation, because the university needs to enforce completion and to report response rates, but it never connects that fact to the answers. Those two pieces of information are kept apart deliberately, and the student is told plainly and repeatedly that this is how it works. Without that belief, the data is worthless.

The second design decision is timing and gating. Many universities in Bangladesh withhold a student's results until the evaluation is complete, which is an effective and entirely legitimate way to reach a full response rate. Where the university sets that policy, this feature enforces it, and the grades screen states the reason clearly with a direct link. Where it does not, the evaluation is a prompt and never a wall. The platform implements the university's choice; it does not impose one.

The third is brevity. An evaluation that takes fifteen minutes across five courses will be answered carelessly. The form is short, the questions are the university's, the scales are consistent, and free-text is optional but genuinely welcomed — the written comments are where the useful signal actually is.

In one line. What this feature does

Graduation at a Bangladeshi private university is not a single event but a sequence of clearances. Before a degree is conferred, several offices must independently confirm that the student owes them nothing: the library must confirm no books and no fines, the hostel must confirm the room was vacated and dues settled, the laboratory must confirm no unreturned equipment or breakage charges, finance must confirm a zero balance across every term including the last, and the department must confirm every requirement is complete. Each is a separate signature on a separate form, obtained in person, in a specific order, over several days.

Students routinely lose weeks to this, and some lose a convocation. The classic failure is a two-hundred-Taka library fine from the second year, unknown to the student, discovered on the day the clearance form reaches the library desk — by which time the convocation registration deadline has passed. Nothing about that is inevitable; the information existed all along and was simply never shown.

This feature shows it early and continuously. From the beginning of the final term, the student sees the full checklist with each office's current state, so a fine from the second year is discovered in week one rather than in the final week. As each office clears her, the item ticks. When everything is clear and requirements are complete, convocation registration opens, with its own fee, gown arrangements, guest allocation and ceremony details.

It also carries the emotional weight of the moment, which is worth designing for honestly. This is the screen a student opens the day she becomes a graduate, and often shares with her family. STU-AI-01 · Tutor

P1

In one line. A study helper that answers only from the course's own material, cites where the answer came from, says plainly when something was not covered, and refuses to do graded work. As a student stuck at midnight, I want help understanding the lecture — not an answer I would be ashamed to submit. Full product rules for Tutor, Practice, Smart Class and usage caps live on Shikho Campus AI.

What this feature does

Students already use general-purpose assistants for their coursework; that is a fact of 2026 and pretending otherwise helps nobody. The question a university product must answer is whether it offers something better: an assistant that knows the actual course, quotes the actual lecture, follows the teacher's actual notation, and declines to do the work the student is being assessed on. That is what this feature is.

Course scoping is the core of it. When a student opens the tutor from inside a course, it draws on that course's published material — the slides, readings, published lecture transcripts, announcements and syllabus — and on nothing else. This makes the answers concretely more useful than a general assistant: it uses the symbols the teacher used, follows the syllabus's sequence, and can point at the exact slide or the exact minute of a lecture. It also makes the honest failure mode possible: when the material does not cover something, the tutor says so instead of producing a confident answer from elsewhere that contradicts what the teacher taught.

Refusing graded work is the second pillar and it is a hard boundary, not a discouragement. Asked to write an assignment, solve the problem set that is due tomorrow, or answer a quiz question, the tutor declines and offers the legitimate alternative: explaining the concept, working through a similar example that is not the assessed one, or checking her reasoning after she has attempted it. The academic integrity position is stated by the university, shown to the student, and enforced consistently.

The tutor is a study aid, not an authority. It never states policy, never interprets a rule about fees, deadlines or eligibility, and never says anything about her grades. Questions of that kind are handed to the screen or the office that owns them. It is a good explainer of course content and nothing more, and the design is careful to keep it in that lane.

STU-ATT-01 · My attendance & exam eligibility

P0

What it does. Shows the student, for every course they are taking, how many classes were held, how many they attended, what percentage that is, and — the part that actually matters — how many more classes they can afford to miss before they lose the right to sit the final examination. It shows this from the first week of term, not at the end. If a record looks wrong, the student can raise it with the teacher from the same screen, with the specific date attached.

Why it exists

Yet students typically discover their attendance figure only when the barred list is pinned to the department noticeboard, days before the exam, when nothing can be done. Every year departments deal with the same scene: students who genuinely did not know, teachers producing a register from memory, and a chairperson deciding under pressure. Making the number visible and explainable from week one removes almost all of that.

STU-SRV-02 · Campus service requests

P1

What it does. Gives students one place to ask any campus office for something and then watch what happens to the request. A lost ID card, a hostel seat, a transport pass, a library problem, a medical appointment, a portal password, a request to change a phone number in the records — all of them become a tracked request with a reference number, a named office, an expected time, and a visible status, instead of a walk from counter to counter during office hours the student is supposed to be in class.

Why it exists

Everything that is not teaching or paying still consumes a student's time and the office's time, and it is almost entirely undocumented. A student loses an ID card and spends three days finding out which office issues a replacement, what it costs, and where to pay. One request desk, shared by every office, turns invisible work into managed work.

STU-MOM-01 · Momentum

P0

What it does. Momentum is the student’s private motivation system — the emotional heart of the student product. It measures how consistently the student keeps pace across their courses, rolled over about two weeks. It measures effort, never grade. It is yours alone — never a leaderboard, never visible to the administrator, never used to rank students against each other. A clear hero score, a seven-day trend, a plain “how it works,” and today’s three rings — Absorb, Retrieve, Apply — that reset at midnight. Closing any one ring keeps the streak; closing all three earns a bonus. A missed day reduces the score but never zeroes it.

Why it exists

Product principle: recognise effort, never rank grades. Turning grades into a score to chase raises anxiety and encourages students to work the system; recognising effort builds study habits. A private measure loses its value the moment it becomes a public ranking. Momentum is personal: it belongs to the student alone and is never a comparison with classmates.
